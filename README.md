# Stick Soldiers

A browser remake of **Stick Soldiers II** (Whitespace Unlimited, 2003): a side-on, black-and-white stick-figure shooter where you grab weapons, armour and jetpacks lying around the map and frag your way to the limit. Now with online multiplayer, nine game modes (Battlefield-style Conquest and Rush, and a Counter-Strike-style Defuse, among them) and 44 weapons, many of them borrowed from the Worms games.

Two games on one site: the **Original**, drawn as it was, and **Stick Soldiers Remastered**, the same game in 2.5D, with scenery in layers, solid blocks, lighting and weather (see [Stick Soldiers Remastered](#stick-soldiers-remastered)).

**Play it:** <https://dec3ptor.github.io/StickFighters/> (Remastered straight away: <https://dec3ptor.github.io/StickFighters/remastered/>)

## What's in it

- **Online multiplayer**: quick match, a server browser, hosting your own game (public, or invite-link only), chat (in team modes, names in their team's colour), and bots that fill empty slots and make room as people join (keeping the sides even in team games, where you can also **change sides** from the menu). No game server: players connect directly to each other (see [Online play](#online-play)).
- **Nine modes**: free-for-all, team deathmatch, **gun game** and **one in the chamber**, plus five objective modes for two teams: **king of the hill**, **capture the flag**, Battlefield-style **Conquest** and **Rush**, and **Defuse**, played like Counter-Strike: rounds with no respawning, a buy menu and an economy, and a bomb to plant or defuse (see [Defuse](#defuse)).
- **Battlefield-style classes** (switched off for now: add `?classes` to the address to try them): **Assault**, **Engineer**, **Support** and **Recon**, each spawning with its own kit and a gadget on `G` (medkits, ammo boxes, spawn beacons, and the Engineer's **Builder**, which puts up walls and planks and repairs what explosions broke), with a **Loadout** screen for each class's weapons and upgrades (see [Classes](#classes)).
- **44 weapons**: the original eight with their original numbers, plus Worms-style ones like sheep, the Holy Hand Grenade, air strikes and the ninja rope, and Counter-Strike-style ones: the AK-47, MP5K and Beretta (cut from the original game), a knife, and HE, flash and smoke grenades (full list below). **Supply crates** parachute in with random weapons.
- **Kill streaks**: kills in a row (without dying) earn rewards you bank and use with `X` when you choose: a **Supply Drop** (3), a **Sentry Sheep** (5), a **Sheep Stampede** (7) and the **Juggernaut** (10). Bots use them too. Multi-kills, sprees, **first blood** and **shutdowns** get callouts, the match ends with **awards** and a **slow-motion replay of the winning kill**, and every kill you make hits with a moment of hit-stop, a kill-confirm mark and its own sound (see [Kill streaks](#kill-streaks)).
- **The Hammer**: a pickup on every map that lets anyone build. Put up walls for cover, and planks to make steps, ledges and bridges where the way is usually blocked (see [The Hammer](#the-hammer)).
- **Bots that get themselves out of trouble.** A bot stuck in a pit builds steps out with a hammer or flies out with a jetpack (waiting for the fuel if it needs more), and one that can't get to an objective or an enemy builds its way up to a ledge or across a gap, or flies there. A wall in the way (one somebody built, or on a destructible map the map's own) it digs through with the sledgehammer, a chunk a swing, and it digs its way out of a room it's been walled into. With none of those, it first goes and gets a jetpack or hammer it can reach. Hurt in a fight at range, a bot with a hammer puts up a wall to hide behind. Self-destructing is the last resort, a few seconds after it finds it has no way out of its own.
- **Jetpacks and hammers wherever the way can get blocked**: every map has them placed so one is never more than a few seconds away, even after explosions cut routes off (see [The Hammer](#the-hammer)).
- **All 8 original maps**, decoded from the original game's files: Fire in the Hole, Habitat, King of the Hill, Fraghouse, Sniping Grounds, Fatality Zone, Hells Steeple and Two Towers.
- **11 new maps**, from small to huge:
  - **Small** (4–16 players): **Summit** (a stepped mountain, made for king of the hill) and **Pit Stop** (a garage with three rotating hills), and **Crossfire** (bunkers and three lanes) and **Twin Keeps** (two castles across a moat), made for capture the flag.
  - **Medium** (6–24 players): **Foundry** (catwalks over molten pits) and **Skyline** (rooftops with deadly gaps), and **Kasbah** (a desert town, made for Defuse: see [Defuse](#defuse)).
  - **Large** (16–64 players, up to 8,000 px wide): **Frontline** (trenches, a ruined village and the defenders' HQ) and **Citadel** (from the beach up to a hilltop keep), made for Rush, and **Harbor** (ships, container stacks and a gantry crane) and **Valley** (hills, caves and a river gorge, with 7 capture points), made for Conquest.
  - Every map, the originals included, has hills, flags, capture points, M-COMs and bomb sites for all five objective modes. Picking a map in the setup switches to the mode it's made for and raises the bots to its smallest player count.
- **Destructible maps** (setup → **Destruction**, on by default): explosions blow holes in walls, floors and platforms, so you can blast a way through a wall, drop enemies through the floor, or dig into a hill. The sledgehammer does it slowly, a chunk a swing. The bigger the blast, the bigger the hole (a bazooka goes through a wall; the Holy Hand Grenade takes a big bite out of anything). Floors that spawn points, flags, M-COMs, hills, capture points and bomb sites stand on can't be destroyed, so every mode keeps working; neither can jump pads, or platforms a map maker marks indestructible. Blocks that can't be destroyed are lightly hatched. Bots find their way through new holes, and in online games everyone sees the same holes, including players who join late.
- **A map maker** with mirror editing, spawn loadouts, test play, share links and **community maps** anyone can browse and play. Maps can be **half old, half new**: blocks can keep the original's paper and ink or Remastered's solid look, whichever game is played.
- **Stick Soldiers Remastered**: the same game in 2.5D, picked when you first visit (see [Stick Soldiers Remastered](#stick-soldiers-remastered)).
- **Health, armour, jetpacks and jump pads**, placed where the original had them. Everyone has 150 health (100 in Defuse, as its weapons and money are made for that): guns and melee take half as many hits again to kill, explosions take the same share of your health as they always did, and a sniper or longbow headshot still kills outright. Dead soldiers drop their weapon. Picking up a new weapon only takes it out if you're holding the starting pistol; otherwise it goes in your inventory (the message says which key selects it).
- **Experimental options** (in the game setup and in **Settings**, under **Experimental**):
  - **Bot vision: only what they see or hear.** Bots spot enemies only on their own screen (a view about as big as yours), in front of them and in a clear line. Behind them, they can only hear you: gunfire, explosions, jetpacks, hard landings and running close by. Creeping along crouched is silent. A bot that hears something turns round and goes to have a look, and a bot standing still looks over its shoulder now and then. The default is the classic bots, which notice anyone nearby in a clear line.
  - **Fog of war.** You only see what your soldier can see from where they stand. Everything out of their line of sight is greyed out, and soldiers there (and what they fire) aren't drawn, teammates included; on big maps the minimap still shows where your team is. Sounds from places you can't see show as orange ripples where they came from, or as arrows at the screen's edge. In online games the host's choice applies to everyone.
- **Stick figures animated with the original game's leg animations**. Arms follow your aim at any angle, and the dead fall apart. No original art is used: everything is drawn in code.
- **Bots** that get around every map, go for weapons, health and crates (but not ones someone else will get to first), and fight with human-like aim and reactions: they take a moment to react, flick their aim over and settle, fall behind when you suddenly jump or turn, and only shoot at someone who'd be on their screen. They use every weapon: they lob grenades, call air strikes on you, steer super sheep and set them off next to you. There are 10 personalities and three skill levels (Easy, Normal and Hard: on Normal, a bot standing still hits about two shots in three at a dodging player close up, and under half at 300 px). For huge battles, the bots slider goes up to **4,000** (see [Design notes](#design-notes)).
- **The original game's sound effects**, from the Stick Soldiers Python fan project (see [`web/sounds/CREDITS.md`](web/sounds/CREDITS.md)). The new weapons' sounds (sheep included) are generated in code. The original game had no music.
- **The HUD**: health, armour, ammo and jet fuel sit just above the weapon bar at the bottom, with stamina in a thin strip under them. A ring round the crosshair fills up while you reload (the pistol's magazine, or the wait between shots of slow guns like the sniper rifle and rocket launchers): when it's full, you can fire.
- **Headshots** do double damage (2.5× with the sniper rifle, 2× with the bow), and you hear a hitmarker tick when you land one.
- **A fight tells you what's happening** (in matches and worlds, both games, offline and online):
  - **Your hits landing.** Every hit that lands ticks and marks your crosshair, not only the ones that kill: a soft tick and four white marks for a body hit, the sharper tick and longer gold marks for a headshot (a kill still gets its red X). A shotgun's pellets tick once, and a stream of body hits (a minigun, a flamethrower) no more than twelve times a second.
  - **Being hit.** A red arc round your soldier points at whoever hit you, on screen or off it, and fades after a moment; the screen's edges flash red by how much it took (however it came: a burn, a fall, your own blast). Under 30% health the edges pulse red and you hear your heartbeat.
  - **Being killed.** While you wait to come back, a panel says who killed you (in their side's colour), with what, whether it was a headshot, and how much health they had left; and the camera goes over to them. Lines of the kill feed about you (a kill of yours in gold, your death in red) stand out from the rest.
  - **Footsteps.** You hear other soldiers running near you, louder the closer they are and from the side they're on (an enemy from about half a screen away, a team-mate from closer still). Creeping along crouched is silent. Your own aren't played.
- **Telling the sides apart.** In team games every soldier wears their side's colour as a shirt (and on their head or helmet and name tag), team-mates off the screen have a small point at its edge toward them, and the small map (top right) shows whenever the whole map doesn't fit on your screen, with your side on it. **Team colours** (Settings, Gameplay) swaps the blue and red for a blue and an orange-red picked to be told apart by people who don't see colours the usual way; the sides are still called Blue and Red.
- **Smooth on high refresh rate screens**: the game draws a frame every time your screen refreshes (60, 120, 144, 240 Hz...). It still updates 60 times a second, and everything moving (soldiers, bullets, rockets, particles, falling crates, running legs) is drawn part way between updates, while your gun follows the mouse on every frame.
- **Drawn with the plain 2D canvas** by default, or with **WebGL** (through [PixiJS](https://pixijs.com/)) if you pick it in Settings. Both draw exactly the same picture. **Auto** picks WebGL on computers with a graphics card and the canvas on ones without, where it's faster.
- **Settings** (on the main menu and the pause menu): a full screen with its sections down the side (**Video**, **Audio**, **Controls**, **Mouse & crosshair**, **Camera**, **Character**, **Gameplay**, **Developer**), each setting on a row with what it does, saved as you change it. On a phone-sized window the sections run along the top.
  - **Character**: your skin, how your soldier looks to everyone, online too. **The game's own** (the stick figure in the original, the soldier in Remastered), **Classic** (the 2003 stick figure, in either game), **Remastered soldier** (in either game), **Ninja** (a mask and a headband with its ends flying), **Robot** (a boxy metal head, an aerial and a visor), **Cowboy** (a wide-brimmed hat) or **Knight** (a steel helm with a plume). In team games every skin wears its team's colour (the headband, the visor, the hat's band, the plume, the helmet), so you can always tell the sides apart. Each is pictured as the game you're playing draws it, and a big picture shows yours in both teams' colours. Picked mid-match, it's worn at once.
  - **Controls**: every action's keys, as many as four each, and they can be mouse buttons (middle, back, forward) or the wheel too. Click a key and press the new one (`Esc` keeps it as it was, `Backspace` clears it); **×** beside a key removes it (take `W` off Jump, say, or leave an action with no key at all), and **+** adds another. A key taken from another action leaves that one, and says so. **Reset all keys** puts them back. Everything in the game that names a key (the hints on the screen, the buy menu, the menu's list of controls, messages like "hold K to self-destruct", online too) names yours.
  - **Volume**: 60% to start with (100% is as loud as the game used to be). Also on the pause menu (`Esc`), next to **Mute sound** (`M`).
  - **Graphics**: Canvas (the default), WebGL or Auto. Switches straight away, even mid-match.
  - **Crosshair**: its style (cross and dot, cross, dot, circle, circle and dot, T or X), colour (the game's own, a few bright ones, or any colour you pick), size, thickness, gap, opacity, and an edge in the opposite shade so it shows on any background. A picture shows it on a light and a dark background as you change it; **Reset** puts it back.
  - **Aim down sights**: holding right click with a gun brings the camera in a little and further toward where you aim, like looking down the sights (Slight, to start with, More or Off). The sniper rifle's scope zooms out instead (the **sniper view**), and the wheel switches to the **far view** (below); in a world, any gun with a scope does the same.
  - **Camera**: how far the view leans toward the mouse (**Look toward the mouse**: 100% to start with; 0 keeps it on you), how long it goes on moving after the mouse stops (**Look smoothing**: Off to start with, so it stops with the mouse; it used to be 330 ms), and how long it takes to catch up with you as you move (**Follow smoothing**: 330 ms; Off fixes it on you). They apply straight away, mid-match too; **Reset** puts them back.
  - **Experimental**: the same bot vision and fog of war choices as the game setup, for the next game you start or host.
  - **Frame rate**: Auto, half the screen rate, or a cap from 30 to 240 fps. Auto draws at your screen's refresh rate; if your computer can't keep up, it first draws lighter (Remastered's auto quality), then drops to an even fraction of the rate (144 → 72) so frames stay evenly spaced, and tries the full rate again later (a few tries, so it doesn't keep flicking between rates). Half the screen rate is a steady every-other-refresh. A cap close to an even fraction of your screen's rate is drawn at that fraction (60 on a 165 Hz screen: 55, every third refresh); Settings says when a cap can't be even on your screen. Moving the window to a screen with a different refresh rate is noticed after a few seconds.
  - **Show frame rate** and **show ping in online games**.
  - **Sprint**: hold `Shift` to sprint, or press it to start and stop. The scoreboard (`Tab`) shows everyone's ping in online games.
  - **Mouse pointer**: your system's own pointer, drawn as the crosshair (to start with), or **locked to the game**; and **Full screen while playing** (see [Controls](#controls)).
  - **Developer**: every number the game plays by. That covers movement (run speed, jump, gravity, jetpack, sprint and stamina, the rope), health and the rules, and every weapon's stats: damage, time between shots, speed, spread, range, ammo, blasts, Defuse prices and how bots use it. It also covers the bots (each difficulty's reactions and aim, how far they see, each bot's personality), pickups, supply crates and kill streak rewards. A change is used straight away (health, the bots' reactions and their route maps from the next match), changed numbers are marked, and a search finds any of them. **Settings & setup** has the defaults for everything players choose: what a new player starts with, kept apart from your own choices. That's Settings (graphics, quality, frame rate, full screen, volume, sprint, aiming zoom, keys, mouse pointer, crosshair, camera, skin, ping) and the game setup on the main menu (mode, map, bots, difficulty, frag limit, each objective mode's goal, your team, lives, kills per gun, supply crates, destruction, kill streaks, bot vision, fog of war, and hosting's player limit and who can join). Each has its own control, picked from the same lists as Settings and the game setup (the crosshair, camera, keys and goals have one for each part; keys are typed by name, like `W Space Up`, with a warning when a key is another action's too), and is marked where it isn't the game's own. Beside each is yours, as you have it now. **Pull** sets one default to yours, and **Pull from current game** sets all of them: from your Settings, and the game setup (the match's, while you're in one you started; the main menu's otherwise). **Use in my game** goes the other way, setting all of yours to the defaults, to try them out as a new player would start (the game setup from the next match). **Copy changes** copies everything that's changed, numbers and defaults, as JSON, ready to paste to whoever makes them the game's own; **Copy everything** copies every number and every default (every action's keys too); **Import** pastes them back in (the choices become the defaults, each checked: one that isn't a choice there is is skipped); **Reset every number** puts the numbers back, and **Put them all back** (under Settings & setup) the defaults. They're kept in the browser, at full speed (the game plays at 75%). In a game you host, everyone plays by your numbers, from when they join and as you change them, mid-match too; in someone else's, theirs, and yours come back after.
  - They're remembered between visits. `?renderer=webgl` / `?renderer=canvas` and `?fps` in the address override them.
- **Game speed fixed at 75%** of the original tuning: movement, jumps, bullets, grenades and fire rates are slowed, while the game keeps updating 60 times a second. Jumps keep their height, so every map stays climbable. A hidden speed picker can be switched back on with `SHOW_SPEED_MENU` in `web/src/config.js`.

## Stick Soldiers Remastered

The first time you visit, you pick a game: the **Original** (black-and-white stick figures on white, as in 2003) or **Stick Soldiers Remastered**. After that you go straight into the one you played last, and the menu's **Play the original** / **Play Remastered** button switches at any time. Addresses can say which: `?game=remastered` or `?game=original`, and `/remastered/` goes straight to Remastered. Links to a map or to join a game open in the game they were made in. Everything that happens in a match is the same in both: maps, weapons, modes, bots, physics, settings and online play. Players in an online game can even play it in different looks; invite links open in the host's.

What Remastered changes is the picture:

- **Scenery in layers.** Each map has a look: a sunset city, night ops in the rain, a frozen ridge, a desert outpost, an overgrown jungle, a foundry, a twilight keep, a war-torn frontline, a harbour or a hangar. Each has a sky (the sun or moon, clouds, stars) and three layers of scenery (mountains, skylines, forests, factories, ships...) that scroll slower and look hazier the further away they are. Dark shapes close up (ferns, rocks, rubble, crates) slide past faster than the fight along the bottom of the screen.
- **Solid blocks.** Blocks have depth: you see the tops of floors below you, the undersides of ledges above and the sides of walls, and they shift as you move, as if seen by a camera. They're textured (concrete, rock, rusted metal, stone, sandstone...) and shaded by the look's light, with a lip of grass, snow, sand or tiles along their tops. Holes blown in them get sides too.
- **Soldiers**: still stick figures, but with shading, a helmet in their team's colour (their own colour in free-for-all), a vest when they have armour, a backpack or a jetpack with a flame, an outline so they stand out against any background, and a shadow on the ground. They still fall apart when they die.
- **Light and weather.** Muzzle flashes, tracers, explosions, rockets, fire, jetpacks, jump pads and pickups glow and light up what's around them. Night maps are dark except where something's lit. Rain, snow, dust, embers, ash or drifting motes fall at different depths.
- **Menus and HUD**: dark glass panels over the map's scenery and a modern font. The HUD is the same as the original's, restyled, with shadowed text so it reads over any background.
- **Quality** (Settings, instead of Graphics): **Auto** (the default) starts on High and steps down a level if your computer can't keep up for a few seconds running, before the frame rate is lowered. **High** draws everything; **Medium** fills the blocks' sides with plain colours and has less weather and a simpler darkness at night; **Low** has flat colours, fewer layers of scenery, and no lights or weather.
- **Map maker**: **Remastered theme** (in the map's settings) picks the look for your map; otherwise one is picked from its name, the same one every time. Share links and community uploads keep it. The **Look** tool can also keep parts of a map in the original's paper and ink, even in Remastered (see [Map maker](#map-maker)).

| Night ops | Fatality Zone |

Remastered asks more of the computer than the original, mostly of the graphics card. Everything in it is drawn in code when a map starts: nothing extra is downloaded.

## Game modes

| Mode | How it works |
|---|---|
| Free-for-all | Everyone for themselves. First to the frag limit wins. |
| Team deathmatch | Blue against red, no friendly fire. First team to the frag limit wins. |
| Gun game | Everyone starts with the pistol, and every kill moves you to the next of 16 weapons (pistol, magnum, Uzi, minigun … super sheep, baseball bat). The first kill with the bat wins. Get killed (or knocked off) with the sledgehammer and you go back a weapon; so does killing yourself. Unlimited ammo, no pickups. |
| One in the chamber | A pistol with one bullet that kills in one hit, and a baseball bat that does too. Every kill gives you a bullet. Everyone has 3 lives (1–5 in the setup); the last one standing wins. No pickups. |
| King of the hill | Blue against red over a hill. Your team scores a point every second it has the hill to itself (nothing while both teams are on it). First to the score wins (50–250). On maps with several hills, the hill moves every minute. |
| Capture the flag | Take the other team's flag from their base and bring it to yours; you can only score while your own flag is at home. Kill a carrier and they drop it; touch your own dropped flag to send it home (it goes home by itself after 20 s, or straight away if it falls off the map). First to 1–10 captures wins. |
| Conquest | Like Battlefield: stand in a lettered capture point to take it (about 7 s alone, faster with more of your team, and an enemy point has to be neutralised first). Every death costs your team a ticket, and the team holding fewer points loses tickets every few seconds. Run out and you lose. You can spawn on points your team holds: while you wait, number keys pick **Auto** (the usable place nearest where you died: a point your team holds with no enemies on it, or your base), **Base**, or a point. |
| Rush | Like Battlefield: blue attacks and red defends. Blue arms an M-COM station by standing at it for 2 seconds with no defenders there; red disarms it the same way (7 s). An armed M-COM blows after 14 s, taking anyone close with it. Destroy every M-COM in a sector and the fight moves to the next sector, spawn points move up, and blue's tickets refill. Both teams respawn at their most forward spawn point (nearest the M-COMs still standing), unless enemies are right there. Blue loses a ticket per death; out of tickets with nothing ticking, blue loses. |
| Defuse | Like Counter-Strike: rounds, with no respawning. The attackers (Terrorists) plant a bomb at site A or B; the defenders (Counter-Terrorists) stop them, or defuse it. Money from kills and rounds buys weapons and gear at the start of each round. Teams swap sides at half time; first to the rounds to win (3–13, 7 to start with). See [Defuse](#defuse). |

In the objective modes, kills don't change the team score; your place on the scoreboard is your score (objective points plus frags). Tickets and points to win are set in the setup (Auto tickets scale with the number of players), and **Your team** picks blue or red (attack or defend, in Rush). The HUD shows how things stand, with markers at the screen's edge pointing to objectives you can't see, and any map that doesn't fit on your screen gets a minimap.

Modes live in [`web/src/modes.js`](web/src/modes.js), [`objmodes.js`](web/src/objmodes.js) and [`defuse.js`](web/src/defuse.js): each one decides what you spawn with, which pickups are around, how damage, kills and scores work, where you spawn, and who wins. Adding a mode means adding an entry there. Where the objectives are comes from the map (see [Map maker](#map-maker)); maps without them get some worked out from their layout ([`objectives.js`](web/src/objectives.js)), so every map plays every mode.

### Defuse

Two teams take turns at attacking and defending a pair of bomb sites, A and B, as in Counter-Strike's bomb defusal.

- **Rounds.** Everyone starts each round at their side's spawn, frozen for 7 seconds while they buy. Then there are 1:45 to plant the bomb. Nobody respawns until the next round: the dead watch their teammates. A round ends when a side is wiped out, the bomb goes off (40 s after it's planted) or is defused, or time runs out (a defenders' win). The result shows for a few seconds, then everyone's back at the spawns for the next round. Survivors keep their weapons and armour; the dead start again with a knife and a pistol.
- **Sides.** Blue starts as the attackers (Terrorists, at blue's spawn points) and red as the defenders (Counter-Terrorists, at red's). After half the rounds, the teams swap sides and money starts again. Level at the end, it's **overtime**: $10,000 each, and a team needs four of the next six rounds (sides swap after three); level again, another overtime.
- **The bomb.** One attacker starts each round with it. Hold `E` (or fire, with the bomb in your hands) at a site for 3 seconds to plant it; you stand still while you do. The round clock stops, the bomb beeps faster and faster, and after 40 seconds it goes off: everyone near it dies, walls or not. Defenders hold `E` by the bomb for 10 seconds to defuse it (5 with a defuse kit); let go and it starts again. When its carrier dies, the bomb drops; any attacker can pick it up (`G` throws it to a teammate). Off-screen markers show the sites, and attackers see where a dropped bomb is.
- **Money.** You start with $800 (at most $16,000). A kill pays $300 ($600 with an SMG, $900 with the shotgun, $1,500 with the knife, $100 with the sniper rifle). Winning a round pays everyone on the team $3,250 ($3,500 for blowing up or defusing the bomb). Losing pays $1,900 the first time and $500 more for each round lost in a row after it (up to $3,400); each win brings that back down a step (to $1,400 at the least). Attackers who planted the bomb but lost get $800 more, the planter and defuser $300 each, and attackers still alive when time runs out get nothing.
- **Buying** (`B`, in your spawn area during the first 20 seconds of a round): weapons and gear in columns. Click, or press a column's number then an item's. What you can't buy says why. You carry one pistol and one main gun: buying another drops the one you had, for a teammate. `G` drops the gun in your hands. Walking over a dropped gun picks it up if you've none of that sort.

| | Items |
|---|---|
| Pistols | Platinum $200, Beretta $300, Magnum $700 |
| SMGs | Uzi $1,050, MP5K $1,500 |
| Heavy | Shotgun $1,100, Minigun $5,200 |
| Rifles | AK-47 $2,700 (Terrorists), M4 Carbine $3,100 (Counter-Terrorists), Sniper Rifle $4,750 |
| Gear | Kevlar Vest $650, Kevlar + Helmet $1,000, Defuse Kit $400 (Counter-Terrorists) |
| Grenades | HE Grenade $300, Flashbang $200 (two), Smoke Grenade $300, Petrol Bomb $400; four grenades at most |

- **Guns have magazines** and a limited amount of spare ammo (an AK-47: 30 in the magazine, 90 spare). They reload by themselves when the magazine runs out; `R` reloads early. Guns hit a little harder than in the other modes.
- **Armour** soaks up less than elsewhere, and only covers your head if you bought the helmet (headshots go straight through a vest without one).
- **Grenades.** A **flashbang** blinds everyone who sees it go off (the screen goes white): longer the closer they are, less if they were looking away; teammates too. A **smoke grenade** puts up a cloud for about 24 seconds that nobody can see through (bots neither), though bullets still go through it. The **HE grenade** is a short-fused frag grenade; the **petrol bomb** sets the floor alight.
- **On screen**: each team's rounds won and its side at the top, the round clock (a blinking bomb once it's planted) and who's still alive; your money at the bottom left, with what you just earned; prompts to plant or defuse, with progress bars; who won each round and how. `Tab` shows your teammates' money and the rounds so far.
- **Bots** buy like players do: a vest or a better pistol on the first round, a rifle and armour once they can afford them, saving up (or buying an SMG when the team keeps losing) when they can't, a defuse kit, and grenades. Attackers go to a site with the bomb carrier, smoke off the far side and throw a flashbang in, plant, and guard the bomb; defenders spread over the sites, rush to defuse once it's planted, and fight first, defuse once it's clear. Flashed bots can't see, and smoke hides you from them.
- **In both games.** Defuse plays the same in the Original and in Remastered, and online, players in either can share a match. Remastered gives it its own touches: the dropped bomb glows faintly, the planted bomb's red light blinks and lights up the site (in the dark too), flashbangs flare white over everything near them, and smoke rolls out in soft puffs.
- **Kasbah** (6–12 players) is made for it: a sandstone town seen from the side. The attackers start on a citadel terrace on the left, the defenders in a plaza on the right. Site A is the palace roof and site B the old cistern under it. Three ways lead to them, as on Counter-Strike's classic maps: **Long A**, an open sniper lane over the house roofs that ends in a drop onto A; **mid**, the street, through covered arcades and a courtyard to a catwalk up to A and doors into the defenders' side; and the low, tight **B tunnels**, ending in the cistern. Defenders reach either site 9–11 seconds before the attackers, and moving between the sites takes them 12–14 seconds. Most of the town can't be blown apart (the walls that make the routes, and the floors and stairs they rely on), though crates and cover can.
- Any map can host Defuse: maps without bomb sites get two, around the M-COMs Rush's attackers go for first. In the map maker, the **Bomb site** tool (`X`) draws them.

## Worlds

The **Worlds** tab (Play online, Play vs bots, **Worlds**) is a different way to play: an endless world made of blocks, like Terraria's, to explore, dig through and build in, with the shooter's own guns. It's kept in your browser, so you can leave and come back to it, and to whatever you built.

- **World settings.** Making a world, **World settings** (under the button) chooses how it plays, and it's kept with it (the Worlds list says what's unusual about each; online, everyone plays the host's): **Seeing**: everything on the screen, or **only what you can see**: what's behind blocks is dark, worked out from your eyes as you move (through a gap in a wall, just the strip you can see through it), and monsters out of sight aren't shown (you hear them: ripples where the sound came from, or an arrow at the screen's edge). **Monsters**: peaceful (none, and no raids), easy, normal or hard (more of them, hitting harder, taking more killing). **Game**: survival; **hardcore**, one life (die, and that's it: you can watch, and the Worlds list says it's over); or **creative**, in dev mode from the start (fly, build with anything, nothing hurts you). **When you die**: what goes in your grave. **Time**: day and night, always day (the night skipped, the days still going by), or always night. **Towns and people**, or none: a wild world. **Raids** on towns, or not. And **what you start with**: tools, guns and enough to build; just a pickaxe, an axe and torches; or nothing at all. **Raiding bases**: with PvP on, when players can break into each other's bases (while their owners are on, only at night, any time, or never); and whether **monsters raid bases** at night.
- **The world.** It's made from a seed (a word or a number you type, or a random one): the same seed makes the same world on any computer. It goes on sideways as far as anyone will ever walk (half a million pixels each way) and 512 blocks deep, and is made in chunks of 32 by 32 blocks only as you go near them (half a millisecond or so each, a few for the busiest). Going somewhere new stays smooth: the chunks round you (three either side, one more the way you're going, and the sky above down to the ground) are made ahead of you, off the game's thread, by a worker (`genworker.js`, the same generator from the same seed: what it makes is just what the game would; without workers, a couple each update); the pictures of the blocks are made a few at a time, those just coming on to the screen first, with one a frame from those just off it when there's time, and the pictures of places left behind are used again for new ones (making a new canvas, and a texture, for each cost more than drawing it); the light's worked out straight from the chunks, with where daylight stops in each column kept (it used to look down every column from the top of the sky); and in Remastered, a biome's scenery that isn't ready yet is made a step each frame and comes in once it is, rather than all at once mid-frame. The surface is biomes side by side: plains (where everyone starts), forests, mountains with snowy peaks and pines, deserts with dunes and cacti, badlands of banded clay, jungles with mud and vines, and tundra with snow, ice and pines, each meeting its neighbours with the hills blended. Underground: soil, then stone, then slate deeper down and basalt and magma near the bedrock; long winding tunnels (some reaching the surface), rounded caves and big caverns; coal, copper, iron, gold, crystal and ember ores, the rarer ones deeper; glowing mushrooms; ruins on the surface and cabins underground with supply crates in them.
- **Seas, lakes and swamps.** Worlds made since seas (new worlds; older ones keep the land they were made with) have **seas** between the lands, often hundreds of blocks across: the land slopes down into them over a **beach** of sand (gravel on cold coasts, red sand by the badlands) to a sea floor of sand, clay and gravel, twenty to forty blocks down, with **kelp** forests and seagrass on it. Warm seas are turquoise over white sand and **coral reefs** (coral in pink, blue and gold, and coral fans); cold seas are iced over (holes here and there in the ice). Most seas have a **shipwreck** on the bottom, broken open, its cargo still in the hold. The land has **lakes** (sand and clay round their edges, reeds in the shallows, lily pads on top; frozen over in the tundra, and the odd oasis in the desert), and there are **swamps**: low, misty, full of pools, with dark trees hung with vines (and stilt-house towns). Caves keep away from water, so none of it is left hanging in the air.
- **More lands, and dense forests.** Worlds made since forests (new worlds) have many more biomes: **birch forests** (white birches, flowers in patches), **dark forests** (dark oaks two trunks wide under one thick canopy, moss on the floor, glowing mushrooms, now and then a giant mushroom), **taiga** (spruces in tiers over podzol, ferns, berry bushes) and **snowy taiga** (the spruces' tiers dusted with snow), **old spruce forests** (giant spruces two trunks wide and twenty-odd blocks tall, mossy boulders), the **savanna** (bent acacias with flat crowns over dry grass, under a golden sky), **cherry groves** (pink blossom on the hills, petals on the ground), **flower meadows**, **bamboo jungles** (bamboo in tall clumps) and, rarely, **mushroom fields** (giant red and brown mushrooms, whose caps you can stand on, on violet mycelium). Forests are dense, a trunk every few blocks with the crowns meeting overhead (with clearings here and there), and mixed: oaks, big oaks with branches, birches, tall birches. Under them grow ferns, bushes, berry bushes (berries to eat), flowers, pumpkins on the plains; fallen logs and mossy boulders lie on the forest floors; palms lean over warm beaches. A tree's felled whole, whatever its shape (two trunks wide, bent, curving, branching), its crown with it, leaving its neighbours their crowns. Remastered has the scenery for each: spruce ridges, the savanna's mesas, pink cherry hills, a dark misty canopy, a violet sky over the mushrooms.
- **Water.** Walk in and you're swimming: slower, floating at the top with your head out when you leave it alone, **jump** to swim up (and, at the top, to leap out onto the bank: held, you leap out as soon as you're up), **crouch** to dive. Jumping in makes a splash; under water, bubbles rise off you, and it gets darker the deeper you go (deep down, you'll want a torch). Your **breath** shows over your health while your head's under water: it runs out in about fifteen seconds, and then you're drowning, hurt every couple of seconds until you're up again (it comes back quickly at the top; none is used in dev mode). Water puts out fire. Explosions don't move it. Blocks can be built in water (in place of it; not torches or chests, which would leave a dry hole), so piers and walls go out into the sea, and you can stand on lily pads.
- **Flowing water** (Minecraft's way). The land's water (seas, lakes, pools, wells and fountains) is still: each block of it a **source**, never moving and never running dry. Open a way beside or under it (a block dug or blown away) and it runs out, a step every few updates: **down** first, a falling column to wherever it lands (streaks running down it), then **along** the ground from there, a level less each block (seven out at most), toward the nearest way down if there's one within four blocks (both ways if not). Its top **slopes down** with its level, tapering off to a thin edge, and ripples run downhill along it. Take away what fed it and it dries up, a level at a time, a falling column draining from the top; flowing water with a source either side (on the ground) becomes a source itself, so the sea fills a hole dug in it straight back in. It washes away grass, flowers, small plants and torches (dropping them), carries what's lying in it along, and pushes you gently along the way it runs (falling water pushes you down); it's only as deep as its level, so you wade through shallow water rather than swim. Not into towns or players' bases. A **bucket** (three iron bars at an anvil, or from a smith) carries it: click still water (either hand) to fill it, click again to pour out a source wherever you like, to flow from there (in dev mode, a bucket pours or takes away water across the brush).
- **Ice** is slippery: on it you're slow to get going, slide on when you let go, take longer to stop and to turn round, and sprint a little faster (a sprint-jump off it keeps its speed). Snow isn't. Monsters, mercenaries and townsfolk brake on it.
- **Rafts and boats.** Made at a workbench: a **raft** (five wood, lashed with two leather or two wool), a **rowing boat** (twenty planks) and a **sailboat** (a rowing boat, two wood and six wool for its sails). With one in hand, click the top of any water in reach (a sea, a lake, a river's flow) and it floats there, bobbing; right click it to get in (not while crouching), **left and right row it**, and **jump to get out**. A raft is slow (a little faster than swimming) and holds one; a rowing boat is faster than running and holds two (the first in rows; the other rides along); a sailboat is quickest of all with the wind behind it and slower into it (the wind's the one the clouds go with: its pennant shows which way). In one you can still shoot, dig and build. Flowing water carries a boat along, one with nobody in it drifts slowly with the wind, and boats stop at the shore: they're never rowed up onto land (one left on land stays put). Boats bump: one with nobody in it is pushed aside. Hit one a few times with no gun in hand (an axe breaks it quicker) and it breaks, back into your hands as a raft or boat; someone in it is tipped into the water (with PvP off, nobody can break a boat someone else is in), and a hurt boat mends if it's left alone. Only players get in: monsters and animals don't, and fish swim under. Boats are kept with the world and go with it to a file; online, everyone sees them, and whoever's rowing moves smoothly on their own screen.
- **Ores, gems and diamonds.** In worlds made since veins, ore runs in veins and seams: coal in long flat seams, copper, iron, silver (deeper) and gold (deeper still) in winding veins, each kind rich in its own parts of the rock, and gold nuggets in small nodes. Deep down are nodes of gems (rubies, sapphires, emeralds, topaz and amethyst: an iron pickaxe or better), and here and there a kimberlite pipe rises from the deep with diamonds in it (a gold pickaxe or better). Near the bottom are geodes: hollow, their shells of crystal lined with amethyst. Gems glint, now and then. Silver is smelted into bars; four diamonds (with gold, silver and wood) make a diamond pickaxe, the best there is. Gems are worth a lot in towns: a city's traders pay the most for them.
- **Digging.** Hold the mouse button with a pickaxe on a block in reach (six blocks): each hit cracks it, and it breaks after a few, dropping what it's made of, which flies to you. Harder blocks take longer and some need a better pickaxe (copper, iron, gold, crystal: crystal ore needs iron, obsidian and ember ore need gold). An axe fells a whole tree for its wood. Plants and torches come away by hand. Explosions blast holes, dropping some of what they break.
- **Building.** Pick blocks from the hotbar and click where they go: against a block, a platform, a ladder, or a wall behind. **R** changes how they go down: one at a time (hold to keep placing), or a line, a rectangle or a box dragged out with the mouse and placed when you let go (the preview shows each block, and how many). Walls go behind, to close rooms in. Doors (three blocks tall) open and shut with a right click. Platforms are stood on and jumped up through, and dropped through with crouch and jump. Ladders are climbed (jump or crouch on one). Torches light the dark. **The hammer** reshapes a block each hit: half blocks (low or high) and ramps up either way, for stairs and roofs; on a wall, it takes it down.
- **Your things.** A hotbar of ten (the number keys, or the mouse wheel) and a bag of thirty: **B** (or **I**) opens them, with your coins at the top (click a stack, then where it goes; right click splits it; shift-click sends it between the hotbar and the bag; a number key puts what the mouse is over in that hotbar slot; Sort puts the bag in order). Hover over anything for its card: what it is, what it does (a gun's damage, rate of fire and magazine, a pickaxe's tier, what food heals), what it's worth and what a shop would pay, and what it's made from. Each slot has a stripe in its kind's colour (gems purple, potions red, weapons orange, tools blue...). **G** throws what's in your hand.
- **Weapon attachments.** Upgrade a gun by fitting attachments to it: pick one up in your bag and click the gun (one in each place; a new one swaps the old one off into your bag), and right click the gun to take them all off. A gun with something fitted has a small ⌖ on its slot, and its card lists what's on it. **Sights**: a red dot, then 2×, 4×, 8× and 16× scopes, and the **Hawkeye scope**, which sees out to **500 blocks**. A sniper rifle has a scope of its own (it looks out to 45 blocks and zooms up to 1.8×) until a better one is fitted. **Muzzle**: a suppressor (monsters hear your shots from a quarter as far), a compensator (steadier), a long barrel (+15% damage, reaches half as far again). **Under the barrel**: a foregrip (steadier) or a laser sight (a red beam shows where you're aiming). **Magazine**: extended, drum and quick-release magazines for guns that have one (the pistols), and hollow-point rounds (+15% damage) for any. They're made at a gunsmith's bench (the best scopes from crystal, sapphires and diamonds), and gunsmiths and supply crates have some.
  - **Scopes**: in a world, a gun with a scope fitted (and a sniper rifle, which has one of its own) looks down it as the sniper rifle does in a match: hold right click (with nothing in your right hand) for the **sniper view**, zoomed out and looking further the way you aim, and while holding it, **wheel up** for the **far view**: the camera goes to where your shot would end, the first thing in its way (a monster, an animal, anyone your shots can hit, or a block), or as far as it goes if nothing is, and that spot sits right under your crosshair. Then the camera stays put, so you can move the crosshair onto a far-off monster and shoot it without the view jumping about; wheel up again to move the view on to wherever you're aiming now. What you couldn't see from where you stand, and anything further than your shot goes, is darkened and nobody there is shown, so you can't peek over walls or into caves and bases. A line under the view says which view you're in, what your shot would hit where the crosshair is now ("aimed at Skeleton"), how many blocks out that is, and how to switch. **Wheel down** goes back to the sniper view, and letting go of right click brings the view back to you. A scope makes a gun's bullets reach as far as it sees (the Hawkeye's 500 blocks); an arrow only carries so far, scope or not. A laser sight (and the sniper rifle's own laser) runs out as far as the gun's shots go, so in the far view it reaches the spot under your crosshair. The ground out there is made ready as you look, and monsters near where you're looking stay (and at night, come), so there's something to shoot.
- **Loot.** What you pick up comes up over the map in big, coloured lines (its picture, how many, and how many you have now; gems, potions, weapons and coins stand out). Things lying on the ground are bigger, the precious ones glow, and the names of those near you (and the one under your crosshair) show over them; hold **Alt** to see the names of everything on the screen. Both keys can be changed in Settings, Controls, with every other key. A new character starts with copper tools, the hammer, a pistol and a shotgun with ammunition, torches, a workbench and enough to build a first shelter. Guns use the ammunition you carry (bullets, shotgun shells, rockets, arrows, fuel) and keep their magazine in their slot; grenades and the like are used up one by one. Supply crates hold guns, ammunition, ore, metal bars and tools.
- **Crafting.** The inventory screen lists what you can make (what you can make now first), and finds a recipe as you type. Some things are made by hand (planks from wood, torches from wood and coal, platforms, ladders, a workbench); the rest at a crafting station standing near you, each made at the one before: at a **workbench**, doors, walls, chests, beds, arrows, a longbow and a **furnace**; at the furnace, ore smelted into copper, iron and gold bars, sand into glass, clay into bricks, stone into stone bricks; at an **anvil** (iron bars), pickaxes, axes and the hammer, metal plate, blast-proof bricks, melee weapons, the ninja rope and the **gunsmith's bench**; and there, the guns (from the Platinum pistol to the M4, the sniper rifle, the bazooka and the minigun, which needs ember ore from near the bottom of the world), ammunition and explosives. Click a recipe to make it once, shift-click for five.
- **Two hands.** Left click uses what's in your left hand (the number keys and the wheel pick it); right click can use something else: hold **X** and press a number (or turn the wheel) to put that slot in your right hand, or click the **R** under it in the inventory screen (the same again: nothing). So a pickaxe on left click and blocks on right, say, or a gun on left and a pickaxe on right. The hotbar marks them L and R. Right click still opens doors and chests and wakes beds first (crouch to use your right hand on them instead); with nothing in your right hand it aims a gun, as before. Guns stay in the left hand.
- **In your hands.** Whatever you hold is shown in your hand as itself: a block as it looks in the world, an apple, a loaf, a potion, a gem, a scroll, a torch with its flame flickering; and the thing in your right hand is held in your other hand, down at your side (when the first leaves a hand free). Using things moves you: a pickaxe is lifted and brought down on the block with each hit, an axe chops, the hammer taps, a block is pushed into place, food goes to your mouth a bite at a time (crumbs falling) and a potion is tipped up and drunk, a recall scroll is opened out and read (its runes glowing) till it takes you home, you reach out for doors, chests and beds, a contract is held up, fists punch, a knife stabs, a sledgehammer swings, grenades are thrown, a bow's string is drawn back to your cheek, and a pistol's magazine comes out and a fresh one goes in as it reloads. Townsfolk have their work in hand too (the smith a hammer, brought down on the anvil now and then; the healer a potion, the innkeeper a loaf). Online, everyone sees what everyone has in hand and what they're doing with it.
- **Chests and beds.** A chest (right click it) keeps thirty stacks, shown beside your things: shift-click sends a stack across, or take all and put all in. Broken, it spills what's in it. **Stack into chests** (in your things) puts what's in your bag into the chests near you that have some of the same already. Right click a bed and that's where you come back after dying (if it's gone by then, you're told, and come back where the world starts).
- **Sleeping.** Right click a bed at night (from half past six in the evening) and you lie down and sleep, unless there are monsters near: the screen goes dark, and playing alone the night goes by in a few seconds, the sky turning, till you wake to the morning, rested. Online, the night goes by once everyone playing is asleep (each is told who's gone to bed, and how many are asleep). Moving gets you up; anything hurting you wakes you.
- **Ender chests and shulker boxes.** An **ender chest** (eight obsidian and an amethyst, at an anvil) opens on your own things: the same thirty slots in every ender chest anywhere in the world, and nobody else's; break one and your things are still there in the next (they're kept with you). A **shulker box** (a chest, two gold bars and a gem, at an anvil: purple with an amethyst, red with a ruby, blue with a sapphire, green with an emerald, yellow with a topaz) is a chest you can pick up: break it and it comes away whole with everything in it (its card says what), carry it, trade it, and put it down somewhere else to open it again. A shulker box won't take a shulker box.
- **Waystones.** A waystone (twelve stone bricks, an emerald and two gold bars, at an anvil) is named for where it's put (the town near it, or the land: *Raventon waystone*, *Desert waystone 2*). Right click one for every other waystone in the world, nearest first, with which way and how far; pick one and you're there at once, your mercenaries with you (a waystone needs fifteen seconds to gather itself again). Anyone can use anyone's; online, the host has the list.
- **Recall scrolls.** Read one (a click, either hand) and keep still for three seconds: you're home (your bed, an inn you rested at, or where the world starts). Moving, or being hurt, spoils it (and keeps the scroll). Temples and markets sell them, and supply crates have them.
- **Day and night.** A day lasts sixteen minutes (the top of the screen has the time). At dusk the light fades, and at night it's moonlight: Remastered's sky goes dark and the stars come out.
- **The sky, and life in it.** Clouds drift by far off with the wind (the Original's and WebGL's sketched in pencil; Remastered's scenery has its own), and over the open sea Remastered's horizon has sea stacks, islets with a tree or two, an arch the waves have worn through and, now and then, a tall ship under sail. High above the land, over the highest mountains, is a **layer of clouds** drifting with the wind that you can climb or fly up into: some pass in front of you, some behind, and inside one everything goes soft and white (grey at night) until you come out. **Birds** fly over: gulls over the sea, songbirds over woods and meadows, crows, parrots in the jungle, an eagle circling the mountains, vultures over the desert, herons over the swamps, bats at dusk and through the night; they flap and glide in loose flocks, keep above the hills (disappearing behind trees), and scatter if you come close or something goes bang near them. **Leaves** drift down from the trees (petals from the cherry trees), **fireflies** glow over the grass on summer nights, and **motes of dust** hang in the air of caves. It all comes from the world's seed and the time of day, so everyone in a world sees the same clouds and the same birds going over, with nothing sent (only birds scattering is each player's own).
- **Monsters.** They come out of the dark near you, out of sight: zombies on the surface at night, skeletons with pistols underground (day or night), and brutes with clubs deep down. They go for the nearest player or mercenary, jump what's in their way, and can't open doors or break blocks: a walled-in room with a door is safe, and they don't come in rooms walled in by players. Killed, they drop coins, and now and then something (bullets, ore, bars, a pistol). When everyone's far away they **stay where they are**: the monster is put to sleep there, as hurt as it was, with its name, and wakes in the same place when someone comes back (so a monster you wounded and ran from is still there, still wounded). Fewer new ones come where some are already asleep. Zombies still go in daylight, and monsters raiding a town or a base go when the raid's over. If you die, what you lose goes in a grave where you died (see **Dying**).
- **Dying.** What you lose when you die goes in a **grave** where you died (a headstone, glowing faintly), as the world was made to have it (**When you die**, in the Worlds tab): **your bag** (and half your coins; your hotbar and what you're wearing are kept), the default; **only half your coins**; or **everything** (every coin, every slot, and what you're wearing). What you carry for a quest is always kept. You come back at your bed (or where the world starts); an arrow at the edge of the screen points to your grave, with how far it is, and the map marks it with a cross. Right click it and it's all yours again (what you were wearing, back on), as much as you have room for; empty, it goes. It's yours alone to open for a quarter of an hour; after that, anyone's. Nothing breaks a grave, and it's kept with the world.
- **Mercenaries.** A mercenary's contract (sixty coins and a gold bar, at the gunsmith's bench), used like a weapon, hires one: they follow you with a rifle, shoot the monsters near you, catch up if they're left behind, and never shoot you. Up to three at a time; they're kept with your things and come back with you.
- **Magma.** Near the bottom of the world, magma rock glows and breathes, heat flows along its cracks and embers rise off it. It burns whoever touches it (standing on it, or against it) and sets them alight: monsters too.
- **Seeing.** In daylight, everything; a few blocks into the ground it gets dark, and underground the only light is torches, what glows (magma, crystal, ember, glowing mushrooms) and a little round you. You walk up a block's height without jumping. The top of the screen says where you are (the biome, how deep, how far from the start) and a small map in the corner shows what's round you.
- **Kept.** The world keeps itself every minute, when you pause, and when you save and quit (the menu): every block changed, your things, and where you were (you come back there, or on top of whatever's been built there since). **Export** saves a world as a file (`.ssworld`), and **Import a world** loads one, here or in another browser.
- **Both games.** In the Original a world is paper and ink, outlined where the blocks meet the open; in Remastered, coloured, shaded blocks under the sky and scenery of the biome you're in, with torches that glow. Walking from one biome into the next, its sky, scenery and weather come in gradually over the land either side of where they meet (and fade in over a moment after a respawn), and the weather fades out a few blocks underground. With WebGL too.
- **Dev mode.** In your own worlds (played alone, or hosted), the **`** key (Settings, Controls) turns on dev mode, for exploring and trying things out: fly anywhere, through anything (the movement keys; jump and crouch for up and down; sprint for faster), zoom a long way out and back in (the wheel, or + and -), everything lit, left click wipes out any block (bedrock, towns and bases too) with a brush ([ and ] change its size), right click builds with the block in hand and uses none up, and nothing hurts you. L lights everything (or not), N moves the time on (morning, noon, evening, midnight), V sends the monsters away (and back), H goes back to the start, U up to the surface, O down to the deep, and comma and full stop to the next town west or east (a key you've given one of the game's actions keeps doing that: J is still the journal). Flying speeds up and slows down smoothly (no faster on the diagonal), and the camera stays right with you rather than trailing behind (which, at that speed, left you at the edge of the screen and drifting on after you stopped); zoomed out a long way, the world's pictures are made smaller, so it stays quick. Turning dev mode off inside the ground puts you at the nearest place with room to stand, rather than stuck in it. **Everything's free**: every recipe in the bag's crafting list can be made from nothing, anywhere, with no crafting station (shift-click: five times), and everything there is that has no recipe (ores, gems, food, the guns only found in crates, every piece of gear, quest things) is in the list too, marked Dev mode: type in its box to find anything (a name, or a kind: *gear*, *weapon*, *block*...). The journal's **Codex** gets a **Take** button on everything in it, uniques and set pieces as they drop. Nothing made for free gives XP. Online, a world's creative mode gives its guests the same.
  - **The dev menu** (**P** in dev mode; Settings, Controls): **God mode** (on with dev mode: nothing hurts you and you can't die, falls and all; off, you can), **Invisible** (monsters don't see you or go for you; online, other players don't see you either, and you're drawn faint), flying or walking, flying speed (½× to 4×), everything lit, heal, a level, coins. **The world**: morning, noon, evening or midnight; no monsters; clear the monsters near you; a raid on the nearest town; go to the start, the surface, the deep, the next town either way, or your bed. **Summon**, where the crosshair was: zombies, skeletons or brutes (one, five or ten at once; **named champions**, three or four times as tough; or **on your side**: they follow you, catch up if left behind, and fight monsters for you), a mercenary; **an air strike, napalm, the concrete donkey** (falling from just above, not the top of the sky), **an earthquake**, **a smiting** (a great blast right there) and **a supply crate**. **Gear**: a piece or a weapon rolled in any rarity, magic to unique. And for the host, **the other players**: go to them, bring them to you, heal them, dev mode for them, or send them away. (In a creative world online, guests get the menu for themselves, not the players part.)
- **Online.** **Host online** (beside a world in the Worlds tab) plays it with friends: choose how many can join, whether anyone can (it's listed in the open games) or only those with the invite link, and whether PvP is on. Friends join from the open games (or the link) and get the world as it is: its seed, and every chunk changed (each makes the rest itself), then every block changed as it happens. The host's browser runs it all: what they dig, build, craft and put in chests is done there, and each player's things come back to them. Their things are kept in the host's world, by name: next time they join (with the same name), they have them back. Keep the page open while they play: the world is online as long as it's open.
- **Towns.** Villages and walled cities stand along the surface, each built in its biome's style: timber, plaster and tiled or thatched roofs in the plains and forests, mud brick and sandstone with flat roofs, awnings and domes in the deserts and badlands, log cabins with snowy roofs and stone chimneys in the tundra, and houses on stilts (with porches and ladders) in the jungle. A village is a dozen buildings round a well: the elder's house, a shop, an open smithy (its anvil, furnace and workbench are anyone's to use), houses, sometimes a farm and a watchtower. A city has a gatehouse at each end (stone towers with doors through them, floors and battlements), a plaza with a fountain, a keep with turrets, a market, a three-storey inn, a temple with stained glass, a smithy, a gunsmith's, barracks and houses. Inside are beds (sleep in one and that's where you come back), tables, chairs, bookshelves, barrels, lanterns and banners. Everyone starts beside a village. Towns are levelled into the land, with no caves under them; nobody can dig, build or blast in one, and monsters don't come out near them. The top of the screen names the town you're in, and its name comes up as you arrive. (Worlds made before towns keep the land they were made with: towns are in worlds made since.)
- **Capitals.** Worlds made since capitals (new worlds) have capitals now and then (one always within a couple of thousand blocks of the start): each takes two regions, four to six hundred blocks across, its own name and title (*Greengate, the Crown City*), built in its land's style. A **great gate** at each end (twin towers, a portcullis over the street, battlements, rooms up inside with a ladder, guards with rifles); in the middle a **grand plaza** (a fountain of water with a statue on a column in it, planters, benches, lamps), a **cathedral** (stained glass windows, pews and an altar, a bell tower rising thirty-odd blocks with an open belfry and a spire) and the **palace** up on a stone **terrace** with stairs (four storeys, battlements, turrets and a great tower, the throne room with its lord, a library, bedchambers); out from them the market, inns, smiths, a library, a guild hall, a counting house and barracks, and **rows of townhouses** (three to five wall to wall, each its own brick, plaster, timber or stone, two to four storeys) along **streets** with **walkways** over them between upper floors. Under the main street runs the **undercity**: a street of its own, lantern-lit, with gutters of water in its floor, a cellar tavern, a black market and a vault, down **shafts** from the street above (trapdoors: crouch and jump to drop through; a ladder back up). The whole street walks from gate to gate.
- **Harbours.** A town (any, made since capitals) by the sea has a harbour: a cobbled road down to the shore with lamps, a **pier** out over the water on pilings (lanterns, barrels), a striped **lighthouse** at its end for cities and capitals (a ladder up inside to the light under glass), and **boats** moored past it, dry inside (break the hull and the sea comes in); a capital's includes a great ship, two masts and a cabin. Wells and fountains in new towns hold water.
- **Dangerous lands.** Worlds made since towers (new worlds) have dangerous lands now and then, well away from the start: the **blighted lands** (grey-violet grass, dead and twisted trees under a bruised sky, **thorns** that cut you as you push through, glowcaps glowing in the gloom) and the **ashen wastes** (ash over black basalt, charred trees that give coal, **magma** breaking through the ground, embers on the wind). Their monsters are out by day as well as by night, more of them, and worse: **wraiths** in the blight (fast, and they don't let go), **ash fiends** in the wastes (fire, up close), now and then a named one.
- **Crystal fields.** Rarely, in the mild and cold lands: white calcite under a clear sky, with spires of **crystal** glowing on it (mined with an iron pickaxe or better: crystal for crystal tools).
- **Monster towers.** In every dangerous land, and now and then elsewhere, a tower stands six to ten floors high (dark bricks in the dangerous lands, stone or sandstone elsewhere), each with its name (*Raven Spire*, *The Black Keep*). Inside, a ladder goes up through each floor (one side, then the other); old walls, barrels, bookshelves, torches on every other floor, supply crates on the way up, and on the top floor, a **treasure chest**. Climb it and monsters come on the floors round you, more and worse the higher you go; near the top, the tower's **lord** stirs (a huge champion with its own name, *Skarn, lord of the Black Keep*), and comes again only long after. The treasure holds gold, gems and fine gear; the lord drops something legendary, or its wings. Battlements on the roof, and a view.
- **Sky islands.** Lumps of earth float high over the land here and there (never over towns or towers): grass and a tree on top, stone and ore underneath, and treasure. Build up to them, or glide.
- **Wings.** Found only in the towers' treasure (**Spirewings**) or dropped by their lords (**Stormwings**), and worn in place of a coat: in the air, hold jump (once past the top of a jump) and you **glide**, falling slowly and sailing on fast; crouch to dive, faster. Stormwings beat for a lift too: press jump again as you glide. They're drawn on your back, folded, or spread wide as you glide; online, everyone sees.
- **Doors.** Walking into a door opens it, and it shuts again behind you (right click opens or shuts one and leaves it so). Mercenaries and townsfolk open doors too; monsters never do.
- **Townsfolk.** Every building with work in it has someone there, with a name and a look of their own: villagers and farmers in the houses, the elder, a merchant in the shop, a blacksmith at the smithy, a gunsmith, an innkeeper, a healer at the temple, guards (at the gates, in the barracks and up the watchtowers), a lord or lady in a city's keep and a trader at the market. Who they are comes from the world's seed: the same people whenever you come back (they appear as you come near, and go when everyone's far away, coming back just where they were and as hurt as they were, not back at home; kept in the world's save). By day they go about the town, in and out of their doors, keeping to where they can walk; at night they're at home. Guards walk the streets day and night and shoot any monster that comes into town (and monsters fight them back: a guard killed is back a few hours later). Nothing else hurts townsfolk (shots go through them), and they hurt nobody else. Their name tags say what they do, and the map shows them in gold.
- **Talking.** Point at someone and **right click** (with anything in your hands): they stop, face you, and say something (by what they do, the time of day and their town). Then whatever they can do for you, on buttons (or the number keys): **Trade** with a shopkeeper; **Heal me** at a healer (the price goes by how hurt you are); **Rest here** at an inn (you're healed, you'll come back there if you die, and playing alone at night you sleep till morning); **Hire a guard** at a city's barracks (they come with you, like a mercenary); and **Any news?**, for word of the next towns either way and what's to be found deep down. **Esc** says goodbye, and walking off ends it.
- **Trading.** Each shop deals in its own things: the general store in a bit of everything (and buys anything, cheaply), the smithy in bars, ore, tools and blades, the gunsmith's in guns, ammunition and explosives, the inn and the farm in food, the temple in potions, and a city's market in gems, silver, gold, rare blocks and, now and then, something rare (a minigun, a holy hand grenade, a crystal pickaxe...). What's for sale changes every day, and what's bought stays sold till the next; cheap things come in lots. Prices go by what things are worth (what they're made of, and the work), a little dearer in cities: shops pay well under what they sell for, so there's no getting rich buying and selling, but gems, gold and good finds fetch plenty. Shops sell gear too, rolled fresh each day (never legendary, set or unique: those are found): the smithy armour, the gunsmith's guns with affixes of their own, the market rings and amulets, the general store a piece of anything; each is that very piece, in its rarity's colour, with its card. Click something for sale to buy a lot; click one of your things to sell one (shift-click: the whole stack).
- **Quests.** Townsfolk have work for whoever asks: a gold **!** over someone means they've something for you, so right click on them and ask **Any work?** Each offers a couple of quests at a time, by the work they do, changing every couple of days: cull the zombies, skeletons or brutes; clear a cave (where it is, and how deep, they tell you); hunt down a named monster at its lair (bigger and meaner than most, with a price on its head); bring ore, bars, gems, food, firewood, herbs or goods from another land; make something for a smith or a gunsmith; mine it yourself; carry a parcel or a letter to someone in another town; go and see a town; ask someone in another town about something; dig down to a depth; find something lost in a chest underground; find an old miner's journal deep down. Twenty kinds, each with its own words, things and numbers, from every townsperson in every town, and harder ones as you go up in level: thousands of different quests (over 1,700 just round the start of a world). When a quest's done, go back to whoever gave it (a delivery: to whoever it's for; a green **?** is over them) and hand it in, for coins, experience and sometimes something more (potions, gems, ammunition, and now and then a piece of gear: a named monster's hunt always pays in gear, each part of the story gives a piece, better as it goes, and the end of someone's own mission a rare one, of the kind they'd know about: armour from a smith, a weapon from a gunsmith or a guard, a ring or an amulet from a trader or a healer). Up to twelve at a time.
- **Their own stories.** Everyone in every town has a life of their own, from the world's seed: where they're from (born here, or come from another town years ago), what they did before (a soldier in the border wars, a caravan driver, a miner until the cave-in...), who they share a house with, family by name in another town (real people: go and meet them), what kind of person they are, what they're afraid of, and what they want most. Ask them (**Tell me about yourself**) and they'll tell you, in their own words.
- **Their own missions.** What they want is a mission of three steps, one following on from the next (a blue badge in their offers): carry a letter to their cousin in another town, bring back the reply, then take them a gift; find what they lost in the caves, kill the skeletons that drove them off, then have something made for you; bring down the brute that killed their father (it has a name, and a lair); fetch the gold, sapphire and silver for a smith's masterpiece (and take the masterpiece); go to the bottom of the world for them; collect the history of the towns round about; find the cure for the winter sickness; make their fortune. Each step pays, the last one best, and each comes with what they say about it.
- **The story: the Ember King.** Every world has the same story, told by people in its own towns (a purple **★** over whoever has the next part): the ground under the starting village shakes at night, and its elder sends you down to see why. A lost miner's journal deep below speaks of black iron that burns and a king in the fire; the village smith makes you a pick fit for the deep stone; the elder's letter takes you to the lord of the nearest city, who wants proof you can fight; the city's trader has the old maps, which show three ancient seals: one in a forgotten chest deep to the west, one in the bones of the Warden to the east, one on the temple's altar (for a diamond). Set in gold by the smith, they make the Ember key; and at the bottom of the world, under the village, the Ember King waits in the fire. Thirteen parts, and then home.
- **Following a quest.** The one you follow shows at the top left: its goals and how far along they are, and which way to go (an arrow and how far: 120 east, 40 down), with a mark on the spot when it's on the screen and an arrow at the edge of the screen when it isn't.
- **Levels and perks.** Experience comes from killing monsters (more for brutes, a lot for a named one), mining ore and gems (more for the rarer ones), making things worth something, finding a new town or biome, going deeper than you've been before, and above all quests. Each level (up to 50) gives a little more health and a perk point: **Tough** (more health), **Miner** (dig faster), **Marksman** (guns hit harder), **Brawler** (so do melee weapons), **Haggler** (shops sell cheaper and pay more), **Scavenger** (more coins and loot from monsters), **Second wind** (heal over time, out of a fight) and **Prospector** (now and then twice the ore or gem), five ranks each. The bar over the hotbar shows your level and how far to the next; a level up is said across the screen.
- **The journal.** **J** opens it: your quests (who gave each, what they said, its goals and reward; follow one, or give it up), your character (your level and experience, your perks, with points to spend, and what you've done: monsters killed, ore and gems mined, things made, quests done, towns found, how deep you've been), and the **codex**: all 318 things there are to have (every block, material, tool, weapon, food, base of gear, unique and set piece), each with how it's had (mined from what, made where and from what, sold by whom, dropped by which monsters, found where), searchable and by kind, with what you've found ticked (anything that's been in your hands counts) and how many of them you've found. Your level, perks and quests are kept with your things; online, the host keeps them for each player, and the townsfolk's work, the marks over them and your journal all work the same.
- **Raids.** Now and then at night, the dead come for a town where players are: a horde gathers at its edges (zombies, skeletons, and brutes for the strong) and comes for the guards and anyone outside, while the townsfolk shut themselves in. Everyone near is told (**Raid on Oakford!**, and how many are left, at the top of the screen); when the last raider falls, everyone who fought is thanked, in coins and experience by how many they killed, and a piece of gear (magic at least: the more raiders killed, the better its chances of being good). Not beaten by dawn, the dead go back into the ground, and nobody's thanked.
- **Playing together.** In a world online, right click on another player for what you can do with them (with a gun in hand in a PvP world, right click aims, as ever). **Invite them into your party** (they right click on you to say yes), up to six: a monster killed near a party member counts for their quests too, and gives them half its experience; the journal's **Share** gives a quest to the rest of the party (each fresh); the HUD shows each other's level and health, and the map shows each other in light blue. **Trade**: ask, and when they ask back you're trading: click your things to put them on the table (and coins), see theirs, and when you've both accepted what's there (any change, and both accept again) they're swapped over.
- **Gear and loot.** Gear is worn in eight places (head, amulet, body, hands, waist, two rings and feet), in 57 bases from a leather cap to an ember helm (some always give something of their own: a circlet magic find, a bone helm life leech, ember plate fire resistance), and drops as Diablo 2 had it, in seven rarities, its name in their colour: **basic** (white: just the base), **magic** (blue: one or two affixes, named for them: *Hale Iron helm of Mending*), **rare** (yellow: three or four, and a name of its own: *Grim Shroud*), **epic** (purple: four or five, rolled bigger), **legendary** (orange: five or six, the biggest, a title, *Doom Shroud of the Deep*, and a power: big critical hits, life leech, thorns, healing, magic find or experience), **set** (green: a piece of one of twelve sets, *Watchman's Vigil*, *Deepdelver's Kit*, *Ironclad*, *Nightstalker*, *Gunslinger's Rig*...; the more of a set worn, the more it gives) and **unique** (gold: 51 of them, each one of a kind and always the same, like *The Gravedigger's Hood*, *Bonecrusher*, *Crown of Thorns* or *Starfall*). What it can give: armour (less damage taken), health, gun and melee damage, critical hits (double damage), life leech (damage done heals you), thorns (hits back whoever hits you up close), faster digging, more coins from monsters, magic find (better drops), healing over time, more experience, and fire resistance (against magma). **Weapons drop with affixes too**, every gun, bow and blade (twelve of the uniques are weapons, from *The Widowmaker* to *The Ember King's Maul*): more damage, a faster rate of fire, more accuracy, and for the pistols a bigger magazine and faster reloading, each only where the weapon has the thing; held, it fires as its card says, and its crits and leech count while it's in your hand. Each piece has an item level, from how deep it dropped and what dropped it: deeper, better bases and bigger numbers. Monsters drop it now and then (brutes most), named monsters always (rare or better), supply crates often; the Warden and the Ember King each drop a unique of their own (and the King his maul). Shops sell it, quests and raids give it. In the inventory (**B**), right click a piece of gear to wear it (a ring goes on whichever hand's free), click what's worn to take it off; hover over anything for its card, and the top of the screen adds up what you're wearing. Shops buy gear (the smithy armour; the market rings and amulets; the gunsmith's guns; the general store anything) for what it is; trade it with other players (it shows what it is on the table); and it's kept with your things.
- **Hunger.** You have food as well as health (twenty points, a bar over your health): everything tiring uses it up, sprinting and jumping, swimming, digging, fighting and being hurt, healing, and a little just from being alive (saturation goes first: how long a meal lasts). Well fed (eighteen or more) and out of a fight for a few seconds, you heal by yourself, faster when you're full; hungry (six or less), nothing heals you by itself, not even perks or gear; starving, you're hurt now and then, down to half your health on easy, to the last of it on normal, and to death on hard. You come back full after dying. A world's **Hunger** setting turns it off (then food just heals, as it always did); there's none in dev mode or creative, and a peaceful world feeds you by itself.
- **Food and potions.** Eating (either hand, a click) fills you up, not when you're full: apples, berries, bread, mushroom and rabbit stew, berry pie, carrots, potatoes and baked potatoes, a golden apple (it heals too), and meat and fish from hunting, raw (a little; raw chicken can make you sick, hungry quicker a while) or cooked (a meal: steak, porkchops, mutton, chicken, venison, rabbit, fish, crab). Potions heal. Shops, farms, inns and monsters have food too.
- **Animals.** By day, animals come round you on the surface, by their land and in herds: cows, sheep, pigs and chickens on grassland and in woods, deer in forests and taiga, rabbits nearly everywhere, goats on mountains, camels in deserts, polar bears on the snow, frogs in swamps, parrots in jungles, crabs on beaches, and fish in seas and lakes (whenever). They wander and graze, keep out of deep water and away from drops, run when they're hurt (deer and rabbits when you come close), and come to whoever holds their food. Hunting them gives meat, leather, wool and feathers (meat comes cooked if they burnt). Shears clip a sheep's wool (it grows back); fed their food (wheat, carrots, seeds, apples...), two of a kind make a young one that grows up; chickens lay eggs. A polar bear that's hurt goes for whoever did it. Only players hunt them: monsters, guards and mercenaries leave them be. They stay where they are when you go: far from everyone, an animal sleeps where it stood (time stands still for it: a lamb grows no older while you're away), wakes there when you come back, and is kept in the world's save, so your farm animals are where you left them. New herds don't come where enough are already about, asleep or awake.
- **Cooking and farming.** A **campfire** (three wood and a coal, by hand) cooks meat, fish and potatoes, as a furnace does, and lights the night. A **hoe** (two wood and two stone, at a workbench) tills dirt and grass into farmland; plant wheat seeds (from tall grass now and then, or a farm), carrots or potatoes on it and they grow, a stage at a time, twice as fast with water within four blocks. Ripe, they give wheat and seeds, or more carrots and potatoes. **Bone meal** (from a bone: skeletons drop them) grows a crop at once, or grass and flowers on grass. Gravel gives flint now and then (arrows: flint, a feather and wood). Leather makes a cap, armour, gloves, a belt and boots; wool makes beds, carpets, mittens, and wool blocks to build with. Village farmers sell seeds, crops, eggs, wool and leather.
- **More to build with.** Cobblestone, roof tiles, thatch (from wheat), plaster, timber beams, mud bricks, stained glass, wooden posts, fences, lanterns, awnings, banners, tables, chairs, bookshelves, barrels and signs, and walls of plaster, timber framing, logs, cobblestone and mud brick: made at the workbench, furnace and anvil.
- **PvP and bases.** With PvP on, players (and their mercenaries) can shoot each other; with it off, only monsters. A **base flag** (ten planks and twenty stone, at a workbench) claims the blocks within twenty of it for whoever put it up, **and their party**: nobody else can dig, build or open chests there, explosions don't break it (unless it's being raided: below), and no other base can be put up next to it. The flag is the base's heart: it has health (a bar over it once it's been hit), and a **stronghold charter** (gold, iron and an emerald, at an anvil), used in your base, raises it a level: level 2 claims 28 blocks round it and its flag takes 2,500, level 3 claims 36 and takes 5,000. Right click your flag to see how it stands; hit it with a **builder's hammer** to mend it (a stone, brick or plank a hit; not while it's under attack). The top of the screen says whose base you're in and how it stands (safe for now, open to raids, its flag fallen), and the map outlines bases: yours and your party's in blue, others' in red (fallen ones grey, under attack flashing), with a mark at the map's edge pointing to yours (and, with PvP on, others you've seen).
- **Defending a base.** In your own base only: **turrets**, an **arrow turret** (planks, iron and a longbow, at an anvil) and a **gun turret** (iron, gold and copper, at a gunsmith's), shoot monsters that come near, and with PvP on, other players (and their mercenaries) who come into the base; never you or your party. Load one by clicking it with arrows or bullets in hand (right click it: how many it has left), or put a chest of them beside it and it takes what it needs. **Spikes** hurt and slow whoever walks through them, a **pressure mine** blows up under them (and breaks no blocks); neither touches your side. **Reinforced bricks** (stone bricks banded with iron) shrug off blasts, and in a raid only siege tools break them; an **iron door** in your base opens only for you and your party (walking into it, or a right click), and raiders can't dig it. An **alarm bell** in your base tells you it's attacked wherever you are in the world, with which way it is (without one, you hear of it only near it): the top of the screen says **Your base is under attack!** and how much its flag has left. Turrets stop while your flag is down.
- **Raiding.** A world's **Raiding bases** setting (made with it: World settings) says when players can break into each other's bases: **while their owners are on** (the usual: one of the base's side is in the world), **only at night**, **any time**, or **never**. With PvP off, bases are always safe. A base open to a raid can be broken into: its built walls take three times as long to dig, the land under it digs as anywhere, blasts break its blocks, raiders can build there (a ladder over a wall), and its flag can be hit (a pickaxe does a little). Its chests stay locked till its flag falls; then the base is anyone's, chests and all, till its side mends the flag. **Siege tools**, at a gunsmith's and an anvil: a **breaching charge** and a heavier **demolition charge**, put on a wall, a door or a flag, go off after a few seconds, breaking everything round them (reinforced bricks and iron doors too) and hitting a flag hard; a **battering ram** knocks built blocks in in a few swings, reinforced ones too, and batters flags. None of it works on a base that can't be raided now, nor in towns.
- **The dead come for your base.** Now and then at night, while you're in your base (and the world's **Monsters raid bases** setting is on), a horde gathers at its edges and comes for its flag, breaking through walls, doors and roofs in their way (reinforced bricks and iron doors hold longest). Beat them for coins and experience, by how many each defender killed; let them bring down the flag and it falls (in a world where players can't raid, that only stops its turrets till it's mended). Dawn sends them back.
- **More from the world.** Flowers are picked (roses, dandelions, violets and daisies, by their colour; cornflowers and marigolds from farms), reeds cut at the water's edge, kelp and coral fans gathered from the sea, fallen petals under cherry trees, ferns and bushes dug up to plant again, sticks from dead bushes. Wool spins into **string** and **rope**, a **loom** weaves **cloth**, reeds press into **paper**, paper and leather bind into **books**, an anvil makes **nails** and **chains**, and fat from a campfire makes **tallow** for **candles**.
- **Dyes, and everything in colours.** Sixteen dyes, from flowers and minerals (bone meal, coal, roses, dandelions, cactus, sapphire, amethyst, crystal...) and mixed from each other. Dyed: wool, carpets, beds, banners, concrete, terracotta, glass panes, candles and paper lanterns in every colour, and roof tiles in ten. Planks come in six woods besides plain (birch, spruce, dark oak, acacia, cherry, mahogany: a dye on plain planks), and tables and chairs in each. Stone is polished (stone, slate, basalt, sandstone, marble, obsidian) and chiseled at a **mason's bench**, which cuts **slabs** and **stairs** too (they go down shaped: a slab low, or high aimed at the top of the tile; stairs up the way you face); blocks of iron, copper, gold and silver (and weathered copper) come from the anvil. And **windows** that open (right click), **trapdoors** (a hatch: shut, stood on; open, climbed through), **fence gates**, **scaffolding** (climbed, stood on), **iron bars**, **hay bales** and **columns** in five stones. Each comes back as itself when it's broken (a blue carpet, a red bed). Every block is kept with its colour (and its shape) in the world's saves and online.
- **Decorations that do something.** Lamps light: candles (standing on a table, or the floor), paper lanterns, crystal lamps, chandeliers, street lamps, and lanterns hung on chains. A **sign** asks what to write on it as it's put up (right click it again to change it); point at it and what it says shows over it, to everyone. A **flower pot** takes a flower (or a fern, a cactus, a mushroom: right click with one in hand), and gives it back. A **chair** is sat in (right click; move to get up), and everyone sees you sitting. Beds of every colour are beds to sleep in. Paintings hang on a wall (one of eight pictures), shelves hold books and jars, a **cabinet** keeps things like a chest, and things stand on tables, cabinets and barrels.
- **New stations.** A **stove** cooks what a campfire does and more (omelettes, soups, roast dinners, apple and pumpkin pies, cookies, cake); a **loom** weaves cloth, rope, carpets and banners; a **mason's bench** cuts and polishes stone; a **drafting table** draws up blueprints. The crafting list has **tabs** (building, furniture, decor, lights, colours, kits, bases, tools and arms, food, materials), each with how many of its recipes you can make now, and finding by name looks through them all.
- **Blueprints.** A whole building at once: a **cottage** (furnished: a bed, a table and chair, a chest, a candle, a lantern), a two-storey **stone house**, a **watchtower**, a **wall section**, a **bridge** and an open **workshop** (a workbench, a furnace and an anvil under a roof), each drawn up at a drafting table and kept (used again and again). Held, it shows the building where it would go, faintly (red where something's in the way, and what's short or wrong); **R** flips it the other way round; a click builds it, block by block over a couple of seconds with puffs of dust, from what you carry (in dev mode, free). It needs room, ground under it (a bridge: at both ends), and the right to build there (not in a town, nor someone else's base). Aimed at the ground, its floor goes into the ground, flush; aimed above it, on it. Saved halfway, it's finished first.
- **Building quicker.** The **middle mouse button** picks the block you're pointing at: the hotbar slot with it, or it brought from your bag into your hand (Settings, Controls: Pick block). Two more brushes on **R**: **fill** (the space closed in where you click, with blocks, or the walls behind it in a room) and **replace** (the same blocks joined to where you click, each swapped for the one in your hand, the old ones given back). **Z** undoes what you last built (Settings, Controls: Undo), five times back and for three minutes after: a block, a line, a fill, a replace or a whole building comes down, and what it used comes back.

## Kill streaks

Kills in a row, without dying, earn rewards. They're banked (dying doesn't lose them) and you use them when you choose: `X` uses one, and `Z` picks which when you have more than one. The HUD shows what's banked, and how far you are from the next.

| Kills in a row | Reward | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | **Supply Drop** | A crate parachutes in beside you with a strong weapon (a minigun, bazooka, homing missile, Holy Hand Grenade, air strike...) and plenty of ammo. For 10 seconds only you (and your team) can open it; then anyone can. |
| 5 | **Sentry Sheep** | An armoured sheep with a gun on its back. It shoots enemies who come near it (in sight, within about 470 px) for 20 seconds: good for holding a hill, a flag or a capture point. It can be wrecked: bullets, melee and above all explosions. |
| 7 | **Sheep Stampede** | A flock of eight sheep pours out toward the nearest enemies, a few after each, and they blow up on whoever they run into. |
| 10 | **Juggernaut** | 20 seconds of armour (you take 45% of any damage: it was 30 seconds at 30%, enough for one streak to decide a match), a minigun that never runs out and can't be put away, and no knockback, with a glowing outline so everyone knows to run. |

Rewards are on in free-for-all, team deathmatch and the objective modes (king of the hill, capture the flag, Conquest and Rush), and off in gun game, one in the chamber and Defuse, where they'd break the mode; the setup's **Kill streaks** option turns them off anywhere. Kills made by the sentry and the stampede count as frags but not toward the next reward (nor does a grenade landing after you died). **Bots use them too**: the Juggernaut and supply drops straight away, the sentry in a fight or by an objective, the stampede with enemies not far off along the ground.

**Callouts**, in every mode: **Double kill**, **Triple kill**, **Multi kill** and up for kills close together; **Killing spree** (3 in a row), **Rampage** (5), **Unstoppable** (10) and **Godlike** (15), each with its own sound sting; **First blood**; and a **Shutdown** for ending someone's streak of 5 or more, worth a bonus frag where rewards are on. Everyone is told when someone's on a rampage or becomes a Juggernaut, and names show a streak badge from 3 in a row, so there's always a reason to hunt the leader.

**End of the match**: if a kill won it, a short **slow-motion replay of the winning kill** plays first (any key or a click skips it), then the scores with **awards**: **MVP**, **Best streak**, **Most headshots** and **Demolition expert** (most explosive kills).

**Hit feel**: every kill you make freezes the action for a moment (hit-stop; online the picture holds, as the game can't), flashes where they fell, marks your crosshair and plays a kill-confirm sound.

## Weapons

Number keys pick a slot; press the same key again to cycle through what you own in that slot. **The further the mouse is from you, the harder you throw.** Click again to set off a sheep or let go of the rope.

| Key | Weapons |
|---|---|
| `1` Pistols | Platinum (infinite, reloads: what you start with), Beretta (infinite, a bigger magazine, far more accurate), Magnum |
| `2` Automatics | Uzi, MP5K, Minigun (spins up first, slows you down while firing) |
| `3` Rifles | M4 Carbine, AK-47 (harder hitting, less accurate), Longbow (arrows drop with distance and stick in walls) |
| `4` Shotguns | Shotgun |
| `5` Precision | Sniper Rifle (laser, zoom), Magic Bullet (homes in on the nearest enemy) |
| `6` Grenades | Grenade Launcher, Cluster Bomb, Banana Bomb (very bouncy, bursts into more bananas), Holy Hand Grenade (sings, then a huge blast); in Defuse, HE Grenade, Flashbang and Smoke Grenade |
| `7` Rockets | Bazooka, Homing Missile, Mortar (falls, then bursts) |
| `8` Fire & gas | Flamethrower, Petrol Bomb (leaves burning petrol), Skunk (poison gas) |
| `9` Animals & explosives | Sheep (walks, hops walls, click to explode), Super Sheep (flies where you point), Old Woman, Mad Cows, Dynamite, Mines (a blast nearby sets off mines and dynamite) |
| `0` Specials | Air Strike, Napalm Strike, Concrete Donkey (smashes through every floor it falls through), Earthquake, Teleport, Kamikaze, Ninja Rope, Hammer (builds walls and planks: below) |
| `F` Melee | Sledgehammer (everyone has one: a heavy swing that reaches a little further and wider than the others and knocks people back, great for pushing them off ledges. Swing it at a block to knock a chunk out: a few swings break through a built wall, or on destructible maps the map itself; hard, hatched blocks and the floors under spawns and objectives don't break), Baseball Bat (home runs), Battle Axe (halves their health), Fire Punch (uppercut); in Defuse, the Knife (three times the damage in the back) |

A few things that make fights fair:

- **Shots hit at arm's length.** Soldiers don't block each other, so an enemy can stand nearer than your gun is long. A shot starts just short of them and hits, with every gun (it used to start at the muzzle, past them, and miss).
- **Spawn protection is whole.** For the moment after you spawn nothing hurts you, and a blast doesn't throw you either (so you can't be shoved off the map before you can move). It ends when you fire.
- **Who threw whom.** A blast that throws a team-mate off the map is the thrower's to answer for: they lose the frag, not the one who fell, and the kill feed says who did it. (Thrown off by an enemy, it's their frag, as ever.)
- **The last sheep** stays in your hand till it's gone off, so the second click still sets it off; then you switch to something else.
- **You come back out of sight.** A spawn point an enemy can see (within shooting distance, with a clear line to it) isn't used while there's one they can't; among those, the further from them the better, with some chance in it. (It used to go by distance alone: on the small maps about half of all respawns were in plain view of someone.)
- **The starting pistol** does 22 a shot from a 12-round magazine: seven body shots to a kill in 2.7 seconds, with rounds to spare for a miss (it was all eight of its eight, in 3.15 seconds). It's still the slowest way to kill: every gun you pick up is quicker.
- **The sniper rifle's scope is for looking down.** Right click held and feet on the ground, the shot goes exactly where it's aimed, as ever, one shot to the head. Fired from the hip it strays a little (up to 1.5° either way, half that crouched), and fired in the air a lot more (3.5°), scope or no scope: no more perfect shots mid-jump.
- **The strongest weapons take longer to come back**: the Sniper Rifle, Bazooka, Minigun, Mortar, Holy Hand Grenade and Air Strike (and the other heavy ones, wherever a map has them) half a minute after they're taken, rather than ten seconds like other guns. Anything taken from the map shows faintly where it'll be again, with a ring that fills as it comes back, and for the ones worth waiting for (those weapons, armour, a jetpack) the seconds left.
- **Each weapon has its own wait between shots.** Putting one away keeps what's left of its wait with it, and the one you take out is ready after the moment it takes to draw (a fifth of a second), so a rocket can be followed by the shotgun. The same slow gun isn't ready any sooner for having been put away and taken out again. (It used to be one wait for everything: after a rocket, nothing fired for over three seconds.)
- **Jumps forgive a little.** A jump pressed up to five updates (a twelfth of a second) before you land happens as you land, and one pressed that soon after running off an edge still jumps. Never twice.
- **Cluster bomblets scatter first.** A Mortar shell's or Cluster Bomb's bomblets fly a moment before they'll go off on someone, rather than all bursting at once on whoever the shell hit: a direct Mortar hit does about 137 (it was 230 to 290, more than a Bazooka rocket at twice the rate of fire).

Air strikes, napalm, the donkey and teleports go where your mouse is. Weapons are defined as data in [`web/src/weapons.js`](web/src/weapons.js), with their behaviour in [`web/src/combat.js`](web/src/combat.js).

### The Hammer

Hammers and jetpacks lie around every map, placed so one is never more than a few seconds from anywhere you can get to, even once explosions have blown holes in the floors (and hammers turn up in supply crates). King of the Hill's pit has two jetpacks at the bottom. Picking it up doesn't take it out of your pocket: press `0` for it. A green outline shows what a click will do (red: it can't, and why if you click). A click puts up a **wall** (12 × 40, standing on the floor below where you aim: cover for a soldier) or, after a right click, a **plank** (64 × 10: a step up, a ledge, a bridge over a hole); right click again for walls. Each click uses a block: a hammer has 8, and another one adds 8 more (16 at most). It reaches 150 px, not through walls, and a block that would overlap the map fits around it. On a destructible map, aiming where an explosion broke the map repairs it instead: what was there at the start comes back in a 64 px square around where you aim. Nothing goes up inside anyone or next to a spawn point (or, in capture the flag and rush, a flag or M-COM), so nobody can be walled in. Built blocks look like sandbags, and explosions break them, even on maps that don't otherwise break. Gun game, one in the chamber and Defuse have no pickups, so no hammers.

Bots pick hammers up too, and use them for what they're for: steps out of a pit, steps up to a ledge or a bridge across a gap to get to an objective or an enemy they can't otherwise reach, and a wall to hide behind when they're hurt in a fight at range.

During a match the browser's own shortcuts are blocked: anything with `Ctrl`, `Alt` or `Cmd` held (bookmark, save, select all, history, print, `Ctrl`+`Shift`+`A`, `Alt`+`←`...), so holding `Ctrl` to crouch and `Shift` to sprint never opens one, and `Alt` and `F10` (the browser's menu), `F1`, `F3`, `F6`, `F7` and Firefox's quick find (`/`, `'`) on their own. Closing or leaving the page asks first. (Text boxes, like online games' chat, keep their shortcuts.)

**The mouse feels like your mouse.** The crosshair is your system's own pointer, drawn as the crosshair you set up: the operating system moves it, as it does everywhere else, with its usual speed and acceleration and no delay, rather than the game drawing it a frame or so behind. (Down a sniper scope it turns red.) The view follows the mouse as it moves and stops when it stops (**Settings → Camera** smooths it, if you like). In a window the pointer can leave the game; full screen keeps it on the screen. **Settings → Mouse pointer → Locked to the game** locks the mouse to the game instead: a click locks it, the browser hides its pointer, and the game draws the crosshair, moved by how far the mouse moves and kept on the screen; `Esc` frees the mouse and opens the menu, and going back to the game locks it again.

**The controls stay in the game.** While you play, the browser's own reactions to the mouse are blocked whatever keys you hold (`Shift` to sprint, `Ctrl` to crouch): no right-click menu, no zooming with `Ctrl` + wheel or sideways scrolling with `Shift` + wheel, no selecting text, no middle-click scrolling or pasting, and the mouse's back and forward buttons don't leave the page, whether the mouse is locked or not. **Full screen while playing** (or **Full screen** in the menu) plays in full screen, where Chrome and Edge also keep their own shortcuts, even `Ctrl`+`W`, for the game; hold `Esc` to leave full screen.

**Firefox and `Shift` + right click.** Firefox always opens its own menu on `Shift` + right click, whatever a web page does (it doesn't even tell the page), unless the mouse is locked to the page. With your system's pointer as the crosshair, the game lets go of every key and button you were holding when that happens (Firefox's menu keeps the keys you let go of while it's open from the game, which would otherwise keep you running), and the first time it says how to stop it: lock the mouse to the game (**Settings → Mouse pointer**), or open `about:config` in Firefox and set `dom.event.contextmenu.shift_suppresses_event` to `false`, after which the game blocks that menu too. Settings says so as well, in Firefox.

## Classes

**Classes are switched off for now**: nothing about them shows in the menus, and every match is played without them. Add `?classes` to the game's address (for example `https://…/StickFighters/?classes`) to turn them back on and try them. The rest of this section describes them as they are then.

**Classes** is a match option in the game setup: **Off** is the classic game (weapons from pickups and crates), **On** gives everyone a class. It starts on for Conquest and Rush and off for the other modes (your choice is remembered for each mode), and isn't offered in gun game or one in the chamber, which hand out their own weapons. Online, the host's choice applies to everyone. With classes on, the map's weapons aren't there (health, armour and jetpacks are), supply crates still drop, and the dead still drop the weapon in their hands.

| Class | Kit | Gadget (`G`) | Other main weapons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assault | M4 (120 rounds), Grenade Launcher ×2 | **Medkit**: 90 health for you or a teammate | Shotgun / Uzi |
| Engineer | Bazooka ×3, Uzi (150), Mines ×2 | **Builder**: walls, planks and repairs (below) | Homing Missile / Mortar (instead of the bazooka) |
| Support | Shotgun (16), Dynamite ×2 | **Ammo box**: half a kit's ammo back for you or a teammate | Minigun / Flamethrower |
| Recon | Sniper Rifle (15) | **Spawn beacon**: you and your team can spawn on it | Longbow / Magic Bullet |

Everyone also has the pistol and the sledgehammer. Medkits and ammo boxes drop at your feet, only your team can take them (one each lying around at a time, for 40 seconds), and they recharge in 20 seconds. A spawn beacon goes where you stand (it drops to the floor if you're in the air); it becomes your spawn choice, and your team can pick it too, with the number keys while waiting to respawn (Conquest's points are still there; the beacons come after them). Enemies destroy it by shooting it (60 health) or blowing it up; if one's right next to it, you spawn at base instead. Your old beacon goes when you place a new one or respawn as another class.

Pick your class in the setup, with `C` while waiting to respawn (your respawn waits while you choose, for up to 15 seconds), or from the menu (`Esc`). Bots pick a class to suit their personality while spreading out over their team, and use their gadgets: medkits when they or a teammate nearby is hurt, ammo when they're low, and beacons near the objectives (they don't build).

**The Builder.** `G` takes it out (or `0`), and a green outline shows what a click will do (red: it can't, and why if you click). On a destructible map, aiming where an explosion broke the map repairs it: what was there at the start comes back in a 64 px square around where you aim, leaving room for anyone standing in it. Anywhere else, a click puts up a **wall** (12 × 40, standing on the floor below where you aim: cover for a soldier) or, after a right click, a **plank** (64 × 10: a step up, a ledge, a bridge over a hole). It reaches 150 px, not through walls, and a block that would overlap the map fits around it. Not inside anyone, nor next to a spawn point (or, in capture the flag and rush, a flag or M-COM), so nobody can be walled in. Each click uses one of six blocks; one comes back every 5 seconds or so, and ammo boxes give half of them back. Built blocks look like sandbags, and explosions break them even on maps that don't otherwise break.

**Upgrades.** Each kit weapon can carry one upgrade:

| Weapons | Upgrades |
|---|---|
| Guns | **Steady grip**: 40% less spread · **Extended mag**: 50% more ammo and a bigger pistol magazine (12) · **Hollow points**: 25% more damage, 30% less range · **Quick hands**: 20% less time between shots and reloading. (Guns without spread can't take steady grip, nor the flamethrower hollow points.) |
| Explosives | **Bigger charge**: 30% bigger blast · **Extra round**: one more |

The **Loadout** screen (main menu, or *Weapons and upgrades…* in the class picker) lets you pick each class's main weapon and an upgrade for each of its weapons, all available from the start. Your choices are saved in your browser (`localStorage`, apart from the settings). Bots play random kits.

## Online play

- **Quick match** joins the fullest open game that has room. If there isn't one, it starts a game for others to find.
- **Host a game** uses the same setup as offline play (any map: originals, your own, or community maps), plus a name, a player limit, and whether it's listed or invite-only. Press `Esc` in the game to copy an **invite link**.
- **Everyone plays the host's game as the host has it**: its setup, its numbers (Settings, Developer), and how it's seen: the host's camera (look toward the mouse, look smoothing, follow smoothing) and how far aiming down the sights zooms. Changed mid-match, they change for everyone a moment later, and players who join later get them as they are then. Your own camera and aiming zoom are kept for your own games (Settings says so while you're in someone else's). What's yours stays yours: keys, sprint hold or toggle, the mouse pointer, crosshair, graphics, sound and skin.
- The **server browser** lists open games as they're hosted, with their map, mode (every mode by its own name) and players.
- **Sides stay even.** In a team game the bot that makes room for someone joining comes from the side with more on it, and bots go over to even the sides up whenever someone joins, leaves, sits out or changes sides. **Change sides** (in the `Esc` menu) puts you on the other side: for a guest, whenever it wouldn't leave that side with two more players than the other (the host says why not, if not); for the host, and against bots offline, always (so you and a friend can play together against the bots). You go out where you stand and come back at your new side's spawn, and the side you chose is kept for the following matches.
- **Leaving.** Closing the tab mid-match asks first; answer "stay" and the game carries on, for everyone in it. The player limit counts those still connecting, so several joining at once can't overfill a game.

How it works, with no game server:

- **The host's browser runs the match.** Everyone else sends their inputs to the host 60 times a second, and gets back a snapshot of the whole game 20 times a second (about 1–2 KB in binary), plus the sounds, effects and kill feed. Your own soldier moves the instant you press a key (client-side prediction) and is corrected smoothly if the host disagrees. Other players are drawn about 100 ms in the past, so they move smoothly between snapshots. Matches are numbered in the snapshots, so one from the last match that turns up late is ignored. That moment follows the host's own pace (a host that slows down doesn't leave them frozen), and is a little further back when snapshots arrive unevenly (5–10 updates, adjusting by itself).
- **Shots leave the gun.** Everyone else's are shown from the moment each was fired, so they come out of the shooter's gun (one isn't drawn before then). Your own are different: you're drawn ahead of everyone else, as you play, so the host's copy of a shot you fired starts from where it had you a moment ago, behind you if you're moving. It's drawn from your gun as it's drawn instead, and joins its real path over its next few updates, so it still lands where the host says it does. Its flash, shell casing or beam is moved the same way. The host sends effects in batches every few updates, each marked with the update it happened in, and players show each at its own, so a gun flashes as its shot leaves it.
- **Players connect directly** with WebRTC data channels. To find each other they use **public [Nostr](https://nostr.com/) relays**: hosts post a listing every 20 seconds, and a player who wants to join sends the host a connection offer, encrypted so only the host can read it. The relays used are in [`web/src/net/relays.js`](web/src/net/relays.js), picked by [`tools/probe-relays.mjs`](tools/probe-relays.mjs), which tests public relays from GitHub Actions (the *Probe Nostr relays* workflow).
- **Limits.** If the host leaves, the game ends for everyone. A few networks (some mobile and office networks) block direct connections; joining then fails with a message saying so. Fixing that would need a TURN relay server, which you can add to `ICE_SERVERS` in `relays.js`.
- **Hosting in the background works.** When the host switches tabs, a worker keeps the game running, and starts the next match when one ends while the tab's hidden.
- **Ping.** Each player measures their round trip to the host every 2 seconds and tells the host, which shares everyone's for the scoreboard.
- **The host's numbers and camera** go out with each player's welcome, and again whenever the host changes one (once a slider being dragged settles: the last of it). Players use them in place of their own while they're in the game, and their own come back when they leave or the game ends.
- **Spectating.** A player who chooses Spectate tells the host, which sits their soldier out (no respawning) and marks them on everyone's scoreboard; they stay a spectator in the following matches until they join again.
- **Classes and building.** Each player's browser sends the host their class and loadouts (when they join and when they change them); the host checks they make sense and uses them from the player's next spawn. Engineers build on the host, and new blocks go out with the holes, in one list of changes to the map in order, so everyone ends up with the same map (players who join later get the whole list).

## Map maker

Click **Map maker** on the main menu, or **+ Make a new map** under **Your maps**.

- **Draw** platforms (`P`) and jump pads (`J`) by dragging. Click to place spawn points (`S`, blue or red team), weapons (`W`), health (`H`), armour (`A`) and jetpacks (`K`). Pickups and spawns drop onto the floor below where you click, unless you turn that off.
- **Indestructible platforms** (explosions can't break them when Destruction is on; they're hatched): tick **Indestructible** under **Drawing platforms** to draw them that way, or use the Indestructible tool (`I`) on platforms you've drawn: click one to mark it (click again to unmark), or drag a box to mark every platform inside it (hold `Shift` to unmark). **All indestructible** and **All breakable** do the whole map. A selected platform has its own **Indestructible** box too.
- **Looks** (half old, half new): a platform can keep a look of its own whichever game is played: **the original's** (white paper with an ink outline, even in Remastered, over its scenery) or **Remastered's** (a solid 2.5D block in the map's Remastered theme, even in the original, on its white page). Use the Look tool (`O`): pick a look on the right, then click platforms (click again to put one back to the game's own look) or drag a box around them (hold `Shift` to put them back). **Every platform** and **Clear every look** do the whole map. A selected platform has its own **Look** box, and **Drawing platforms** has one for new platforms. In the map maker they show in paper cream and in the theme's colours. Jump pads keep their own look, mirror editing doesn't copy looks (so one side can be old and the other new), and pieces blown off a block keep its look, while walls built in a match look like the game played. Share links, community uploads and online matches keep looks; the original game's `.ssm` files can't.
- **Edit** with the select tool (`V`): drag things to move them, and drag the corner squares to resize. Right-click or `Del` deletes, `Ctrl+D` duplicates, arrow keys nudge, and `Ctrl+Z` / `Ctrl+Y` undo and redo. The mouse wheel zooms, dragging empty space moves the view, and `F` fits the whole map.
- **Mirror editing** (`M`): anything you add, move, resize or delete is copied to the other side of a mirror line (purple; you can move it). Spawns can swap blue and red on the other side. **Copy left → right** and **Copy right → left** rebuild one half from the other, and **Mirror to other side** copies one thing.
- **Objectives**: draw hills (`L`), capture points (`C`) and Defuse's bomb sites (`X`) by dragging (or click for a standard size), and click to place each team's flag stand (`B`) and Rush M-COM stations (`R`, with a sector: sector 1 falls first). Capture points and bomb sites get letters A, B, C… (change them on the right). Spawn points can belong to a Rush sector, so the attack and the defence move up as sectors fall. Leave a mode's objectives out and the game works some out from the layout.
- **Everyone spawns with**: pick weapons, armour and a jetpack that every player starts with on your map (in free-for-all and team games).
- **Remastered theme**: the scenery, materials and weather your map has in Stick Soldiers Remastered (a sunset city, a frozen ridge, a jungle...), and the materials of platforms given Remastered's look in either game. Leave it on **Picked from the map's name** and one is picked for you, the same one every time.
- **Map check** runs the bots' route planner over your map. It flags pits you can't climb out of, areas you can't get to, spawns inside walls or over the void, and pickups and objectives nobody can reach. Click a problem to see where it is. Test play won't start while the map has errors.
- **Test play** starts a match on your map with the menu's settings, in the mode picked next to the button. The menu (`Esc`) then has **Back to map maker**.
- **Saving** is automatic, in your browser. Your maps are under **Your maps** in the setup, with an **Edit** button.
- **Sharing:** **Copy share link** puts the whole map in the link itself. Opening the link adds the map to the other player's list and selects it. **Upload to community** publishes it (on the same public relays) for everyone to find under **Community**; uploading again replaces it, and you can remove your own.
- **Files:** export and import maps as `.json`, or as the original game's `.ssm` format. **Open… → Copy & edit** starts a new map from one of the 8 originals.

Falling below the dashed line at the bottom of the map kills you. The map's edges are worked out from what you place.

## Running it locally

Needs [Node.js](https://nodejs.org/) 20 or newer.

```sh
npm install
npm start            # serves the game at http://localhost:8080
npm run relay        # optional: a local Nostr relay at ws://localhost:7447, for online play without the internet:
                     # open http://localhost:8080/?relay=ws://localhost:7447 in two windows, host in one, join in the other
npm test             # game logic: physics, bots, weapons, modes, netcode, lobby (through a local relay)
npx playwright install chromium
npm run test:browser # in Chromium: plays matches, uses the map maker, checks WebGL draws the same as the canvas,
                     # checks smooth drawing above 60 fps, the settings screen, classes (the Loadout screen, the class
                     # picker, building in both renderers), Remastered (the game picker, switching games, matches in
                     # several looks, on the site as it's published), and plays online between three browsers
```

Add `?game=original` or `?game=remastered` to the address to skip the game picker.

There's no build step: the game is plain JavaScript modules in `web/`, and the browser loads them directly. Two libraries are copied into `web/vendor/`: [PixiJS](https://pixijs.com/) 8.22 (`pixi.min.js`, MIT licence), for WebGL drawing, and [noble-secp256k1](https://github.com/paulmillr/noble-secp256k1), for signing relay messages. PixiJS is only downloaded on computers that draw with WebGL.

## Design notes

- **Destruction.** The map stays a set of boxes. An explosion cuts a rounded hole (a wide box, a tall box and a square between them, 40% of the blast's radius) out of every block it touches, and each block hit is replaced by the boxes left around the hole. Holes that would leave a sliver along a block's edge go to the edge instead. Collision, sight lines, fog of war and drawing all keep working on boxes, unchanged. The bots' route map isn't rebuilt (that takes up to half a second on big maps): every standing spot remembers where each move it tried went, and a moment after a hole only the moves that went near it are simulated again (a few ms in all). That's done a few spots per game update, so a hole never holds up the game; bots carry on along their old routes meanwhile, then plan again, a few at a time. A test checks the patched route map gets everywhere a fresh one would. Online, the host sends each hole (position and size) in order, and players blow the same holes, which come out the same everywhere; players who join late or miss one get them all.

- **Jump height.** The original's physics constants are compiled into its program, which this project doesn't touch. Jumps were first tuned against its maps (about 162 px, the least that let you climb out of every pit in all 8), then lowered to 60% of that, about 97 px (a soldier is 34 px tall), which plays better. Seven of the original maps work with it. On King of the Hill the pit at the bottom becomes a deliberate trap: you can drop in but not climb out, so nobody spawns there, nothing anyone needs is down there, bots only go in by accident, and the way out is a jetpack (two lie at the bottom), a hammer, or dying (hold `K` to self-destruct; stuck bots fly or build their way out if they can, and self-destruct only if they can't). The new maps were reshaped for the lower jump. Jump pads launch 2.05× their stored "bounce" value. A test checks that every map is fully connected (apart from that pit).
- **Bots.** The bots' route planner finds every spot a soldier can stand on (not on a jump pad set into the floor: whoever's middle is over one is launched, so stepping onto it is a move from beside it). It then tries walks, jumps (including mid-air direction changes) and drops from each spot with the same physics code the game uses, so every planned move is one the bot can actually perform. A move goes where the soldier comes to a stop: landing at speed by the end of a ledge, braking can carry it off the end, down to the floor below. Bots perform the moves as they were tried: running along a floor, a bot brakes in time (worked out update by update, the way the physics slows a soldier) to stop right where its jump starts, then steps the last fraction of a pixel onto the spot, so it jumps from exactly there, standing still. (Bots used to start jumps up to a pixel and a half off, still moving, and run on past the spot, and about one jump up onto a ledge in five missed.) As it lands it stops steering the jump's way, and landing a spot beside where the jump goes counts. In a real match a move can still go wrong (knocked off course, say), so a move that fails costs more to plan with next time and the bots find another way (for the rest of the match: each match starts afresh). A bot that hasn't got anywhere for a few seconds gives up on what it was doing and goes somewhere else. Standing spots are worked out from where there's room for a soldier, so even a nook a few pixels wide that holes leave has one; a bot that still ends up somewhere with no spot (just before the route map catches up with a hole) tries every jump, drop and walk from exactly where it is and takes one that lands on a spot. A planned move that takes far longer than it should (bouncing on the edge of a jump pad) counts as failed, and a bot bouncing with nothing planned steps off the pad. A bot that can't get to an enemy or objective heads for the nearest spot it can get to, and one that reaches where it last saw someone, and finds nobody, moves on rather than waiting. Pickups behind a ceiling or wall are left alone. Before going for a pickup, a bot works out how long it'll take to get there and compares that with the other bots already on their way (by what's left of their routes) and anyone standing right next to it. If someone will clearly get there first, it finds something else to do, and a bot on its way gives up as soon as someone faster heads for the same thing. A bot that gives way leaves that item alone for a few seconds, and a bot already on its way to an item sticks with it, rather than dithering between it and its objective. Jumps cost a little extra when planning routes, so bots walk wherever walking is about as quick, rather than hopping along flat floors.
- **Bots getting out of trouble** ([`web/src/botescape.js`](web/src/botescape.js)). The route map knows which spots are in a pit (no way back to the rest of the map) and which places a bot can't get to from where it is. A bot in a pit with nothing to do there heads for the nearest spot out. With a jetpack it flies there, once it has the fuel for that height (it fills up on the ground; for a climb higher than a full tank lasts, the jetpack is no help). With a hammer it builds steps: a plank a step up and across, then it jumps onto it and looks again; in a shaft too narrow for a step beside it, it jumps and puts a plank (or, narrower still, a wall) under its own feet at the top of the jump. The route map takes a moment to catch up with new blocks, so after each step the bot tries every walk, jump and drop from exactly where it stands, with the real physics, and carries on building until one lands somewhere with a way on (a long jump up could miss, so it builds another step first while it has blocks). An objective or enemy it can't get to at all works the same way: it walks to the nearest spot it can get to with open sky above (steps can't go up through a ledge), then flies there, builds steps up (toward it first, and never in under a ledge), or lays a bridge across the gap, walking out along each plank and going all the way across rather than jumping the last bit. On a destructible map, a click over a hole explosions made puts the floor back, which makes as good a bridge. With no jetpack or blocks, a bot looks for a wall it could dig through instead: a spot it can get to with a wall beside it (no more than about 100 px thick, built, or the map's own on a destructible map, and none of it hard) and, on the far side about level with it, a spot with a way to where it's going (or, from a pit, out). It walks there and swings the sledgehammer at the middle of whatever's still in the way of its body, nearest first, from head height to its feet, walking on as the way clears, until it's through (the route map catches up a moment later). A built wall takes it a few seconds, a thick wall of the map's own several more. It stops to fight anyone it has a clear shot at. A wall that turns out hard, or 30 seconds without getting through, counts as a failed try. A bot with no way past of its own goes and gets one first: the nearest jetpack (one that can lift it that high) or hammer it can get to, even half a minute away; a hammer lying about is otherwise just something to pick up on the way past, not a gun to go out of its way for. Only a bot with no way out of its own and none to fetch (no hammer, a jetpack that can't lift it that high, no wall to dig through, or three failed tries) self-destructs, about four seconds after it's stuck with nobody to fight (going for something to pick up down there doesn't stop the clock). In a fight at range, hurt or reloading, a bot with blocks puts up a wall a step in front of it, standing still while it does, and stays behind it for a moment.
- **Where the jetpacks and hammers go.** Each map was checked by blowing random holes in it, as a long fight would, and seeing which places get cut off from where everyone spawns, and how far every spot is from a jetpack or hammer. Pairs were added (one each side, on mirrored maps) until every spot anyone can get to has one within about nine seconds' travel, and the places holes cut off can still get to one. A test checks every map stays that way.
- **Bot aim.** Bots aim like someone with a mouse. A bot aims at where it saw its target a moment ago (about a fifth of a second on Normal), carried on part of the way at the speed they were going, so a sudden jump or turn throws it off until it catches up. On spotting someone it flicks its aim over, landing off by an amount that grows with how far it had to turn, then settles to a steady wobble (shakier while it's moving or in the air, and jolted when it's hit). It waits a moment before its first shot (half that when switching between enemies mid-fight), and holds fire while its aim is visibly off. It only shoots at someone who'd be on its screen if it were a player (zoomed out with a sniper rifle's scope), so you're never shot by a bot you couldn't have seen. The difficulty levels set the reaction time, delay, flick and wobble; a test checks they stay in order and miss a fair bit.
- **Floating pickups.** Items sit exactly where the original map files put them, which is often 20–50 px above the floor. You may need to hop to grab some.
- **Huge battles.** Up to about 1,000 bots run at full speed on a typical computer, and 2,000 on a fast one. Beyond that the game runs in slow motion instead of freezing (`?fps` or Settings → Show frame rate shows the game speed). Games with more than 32 soldiers keep a grid of who's where (`grid.js`), so bots, bullets, flames and explosions only check soldiers nearby. Bots look at the nearest few soldiers instead of everyone, and route searches, spawn safety and similar work are shared between bots. Only on-screen soldiers are drawn, bots' names are hidden in a crowd, and the scoreboard shows the top 20. Smaller games don't use any of this, so they play exactly as before. `tests/crowd.test.mjs` checks the shortcuts give the same answers as checking everyone.
- **Smoothness.** Nothing big happens in a single update: route map patches are spread over several updates, at most one bot in 20 plans its route in any one update (others wait an update or two), and route searches run on flat number arrays. The busy code makes almost no garbage, so the browser seldom has to stop and clean up (each stop is a dropped frame): every soldier and every particle has one fixed shape, lookups fill lists kept for the purpose (a map lookup answers with one reused record, nearby soldiers are found into reused lists), stick figures' poses are kept per soldier and filled in place, and distances use a garbage-free copy of `Math.hypot` (the browser's makes garbage on every call). Apart from the planning limit, seeded matches play out exactly as before, which was checked update by update. Drawing sets the canvas up once for all the soldiers, skips particles and stains off screen, sorts the scoreboard only when the order changes and remakes texts only when they change. Remastered redraws the night's darkness only when something in it changes and, without a graphics card, copies a still scenery and the vignette instead of drawing them again. The canvas draws only the part of the map that's on screen. (Keeping the map in 512-pixel tiles and copying them each frame, `maptiles.js`, was tried: quicker without a graphics card, but about 20% slower with one, so it's only used with `?tiles=on` in the address.) The minimap's blocks are kept in a picture too, the world is drawn at whole screen pixels, and the canvas is opaque, so the browser doesn't blend it with the page.
- **Frame rate.** The game itself always updates 60 times a second (so physics, bots and online play are the same for everyone), and frames are drawn as often as the screen refreshes. Each thing remembers where it was at the last update, and frames draw it the right fraction of the way to where it is now. The camera eases by elapsed time, so it moves the same at 60 or 240 frames a second.
- **WebGL.** All drawing code is written for the browser's 2D canvas. `glctx.js` takes the same drawing calls and turns them into triangles itself (with PixiJS's own line and fill rules, round ends and joins included), in reused buffers drawn as a few meshes between the cached texts, in the same order, so the WebGL renderer reuses every drawing (soldiers, weapons, animals, the HUD) unchanged. (It used to hand each shape to PixiJS, which built it again every frame: drawing took three to four times as long and made twenty times the garbage.) The map is built once, and blood and most particles are drawn as one batch each, as they're the most numerous. A browser test draws the same frames both ways and checks they match. Computers without a graphics card run WebGL on the processor, which is slower than the canvas, so those get the canvas.
- **Objective modes.** Everything a mode tracks (flags, capture progress, M-COM fuses) is plain data in `game.modeState`, which the host sends in every snapshot, so players see the same thing without any extra messages. Bots ask the mode where they should be (a hill, a flag, the point they've picked, an M-COM), then go there and hold it: they move about inside the area and fight from there, and on the way they sometimes stop to fight. Roles come from each bot's id, so some defend and some attack, and they spread over several objectives instead of crowding one. Rush defenders guard an M-COM from a few steps away rather than standing on it, so it can still be armed.
- **Classes.** Kits replace the mode's loadout at spawn (`applyKit`), so with classes off nothing changes. Weapon upgrades never touch the weapon definitions: `weaponFor(s, id)` gives a view of the weapon with the soldier's upgrade applied (a bigger magazine, a scaled blast...), which firing, reloading, pickups and the HUD use; it reads through to the real numbers, so it follows the game speed. Gadget timers are written like weapon numbers, in updates at full speed, and stretched by the game speed (a 15 s recharge is 20 s at 75%). Spawn beacons are plain data in `game.beacons`, sent in snapshots; shots and blasts reach them through small calls next to the hit checks and `explode()`, which is left as it is.
- **Building.** Built blocks are ordinary map boxes, marked as built (drawn as sandbags, and breakable on any map). Repairs fill in what's missing of the map as it was at the start (a copy is kept) around where you aim, less whatever's there now and anyone standing there; repaired blocks are plain map again. Each new block patches the bots' route map just as a hole does, and online it goes in the same ordered list as the holes, so block numbers line up for everyone. Bots walk on, around and over built blocks but don't build themselves.
- **Remastered.** It's a second renderer, so nothing about the game changes: `RemasteredRenderer` extends the original's canvas renderer, keeping its camera, objectives, pickups and rarer effects, and draws the rest its own way. A block's back is its front shrunk 7% toward a vanishing point a little above the middle of the screen, and only its open edges (not touching another block) get sides; which edges are open is worked out once per block, and again only near a hole or a new wall. Sides are drawn furthest from the vanishing point first, then the fronts. Each scenery layer is drawn once into a strip that repeats sideways; every frame the strips are copied into a picture at 45–75% of the screen's resolution and stretched over the screen in one go, and when the camera hasn't moved, last frame's picture is used again. Textures are small repeating pictures made in code and laid in world units, so neighbouring blocks join up seamlessly. Night darkness is drawn at a quarter of the resolution with the lights cut out of it. The HUD is the original's code, drawn through a stand-in for the canvas that swaps colours and fonts as they're set, so both games always have the same HUD.
- **Defuse.** The rounds, money, gear and the bomb are plain data in `game.modeState`, like the other objective modes', so online players see them with nothing extra: the host runs the rules and players only draw them. Buying is the one thing players send (a message the host checks as it would its own: money, buy zone, buy time, side). The mode has a few more hooks than the others: it can hold soldiers still or change their input (the freeze, planting), say what `E` does where you stand, decide what you can pick up, and drop what it likes when you die. Shop weapons are views of the usual weapon (`weaponFor`, as for class upgrades) with a magazine, spare ammo and Counter-Strike's prices, so the other modes' weapons are unchanged. Bots ask the mode where to be (as in the other objective modes), when to hold `E`, and where to throw flashbangs and smoke.
- **Same game everywhere.** Offline, hosting and joining all run the same `Game` code. The host records every sound and effect as it happens and sends them on, and players draw trails (rocket smoke, jetpack flames) themselves, from what's in the snapshots.

- **Kill streaks.** The host counts streaks and decides everything, as for frags: players send which reward `X` used, and the snapshots carry what each player has banked, who's a Juggernaut and everyone's streak. Callouts are a separate kind of event (to one player, or news for everyone), and the awards and the winning kill go in the snapshot once the match is over. The Sentry Sheep is a projectile (as sheep are), so it reaches players like any other; its armour and team ride in fields projectiles already had. Durations are in real seconds (20 s and 30 s whatever the game speed); the sentry's numbers are a hidden weapon's, so its fire rate and bullets follow the game speed like every gun's.
- **Looks of their own (mixed maps).** A platform's `look` ('classic' or 'remastered', never on jump pads) goes into its block (`GameMap.solids[].look`; pieces carved off keep it, built blocks have none), and into share links as a seventh number per platform (old links and old copies of the game just leave it out). Each game draws the blocks without one its own way, and the others the other game's way: the original draws blocks given Remastered's look with Remastered's `Blocks` (in the map's theme's materials, at medium quality: plain sides, and plain fronts with WebGL, which can't repeat a picture), then its paper ones over them, so their ink outline shows against the solid ones; Remastered draws its solid blocks, then the paper ones with the original's `paintMap`. A block's sides only show along edges no other block covers, whatever that block's look. Seams (the hairlines hidden where blocks meet) are filled in only between paper blocks, worked out for each game's own look. The original's map tiles (`?tiles=on`) aren't used on maps with blocks given Remastered's look: their sides move with the camera.
- **Skins.** A soldier's `skin` is one of `skins.js`'s; each game draws `auto` its own way. The original draws stick-figure skins with its own figure code, only the head changing (`drawSkinHead`), and Remastered's soldier with Remastered's code, on paper ink. Remastered draws stick-figure skins in its theme's ink with its soft outline (`drawLineSoldier`), close round the head's own shape (`drawHeadHalo`), not round a hat or a plume. Where the canvas can't do something (WebGL's stand-in can't round corners or fade colours), the drawing does without. Online, a player sends their skin to the host, which keeps it for them across matches and puts everyone's in the roster (bots wear the game's own).
- **Players' choices in the developer menu** (`gamedefaults.js`). The defaults being made are kept in the browser apart from your own settings (`stick-soldiers-dev-defaults`: just those changed from the game's own, `main.js`'s `DEFAULTS`). Each choice has an editor (made in `main.js`): one of a list (the page's own menus are the lists, so the options are the ones players get), on or off, a number (kept in range and to the slider's step), a colour, an object of parts (the crosshair, the camera; the objective modes' goals, keeping just those not at their mode's own), or the keys (just the actions changed from `keys.js`'s own, each a list of keys that can be bound). The same editor checks every way a default comes in, whether changed in its control, pulled from your game or pasted in, so nothing that isn't a choice there is gets kept (one of your own maps can't be a default, say). Copied out, they go in the same JSON as the numbers, as `settings` and `setup`, compared with the game's own (objects key by key). **Use in my game** saves them as your settings and uses them just as at start-up. Not copied: your name, your online game's name, the game you played last, and the game speed (fixed at 75%).
- **Tuning (the developer menu).** `tuning.js` changes the numbers `speed.js` keeps at full speed (its `BASE_VALUES`, copied from `config.js` and `weapons.js` when the page loads), then has it scale them again for the game's speed, so a change shows at once and lasts through new matches; bots' difficulty, sight and personalities, pickups' respawn times, crates and kill streak thresholds are changed where they live. It keeps a copy of every number as the game had it, so applying a set of changes always starts from the game's own. Only numbers it lists can change (nothing pasted in reaches anything else), each kept in range (health at most 255: it goes online in a byte). The host sends its changes with each match's welcome, and players use them for that match, since everyone's movement has to match for their own soldier's movement to be predicted; they're back to their own for their own games. The bots' route maps are cached by the numbers in use as well as the map and speed.
- **The replay.** The last seven seconds are kept as they happen: offline and on the host, each update's soldiers and projectiles (as small plain copies) and the sounds and effects made in it; online players keep the host's snapshots and events, which already hold all of that. It plays on a copy of the game made for drawing (its own soldiers, projectiles and effects), so the real match isn't touched while the host goes on sending it. It eases into slow motion (about a fifth of normal speed) for the moment of the kill, with the camera gliding to take in both soldiers and zooming in a little. Games with more than 120 soldiers aren't recorded (too much to copy every update).
