# Stick Soldiers

A browser remake of **Stick Soldiers II** (Whitespace Unlimited, 2003): a side-on, black-and-white stick-figure shooter where you grab weapons, armour and jetpacks lying around the map and frag your way to the limit. Now with online multiplayer, nine game modes (Battlefield-style Conquest and Rush, and a Counter-Strike-style Defuse, among them) and 44 weapons, many of them borrowed from the Worms games.

Two games on one site: the **Original**, drawn as it was, and **Stick Soldiers Remastered**, the same game in 2.5D, with scenery in layers, solid blocks, lighting and weather (see [Stick Soldiers Remastered](#stick-soldiers-remastered)).

**Play it:** <https://dec3ptor.github.io/StickFighters/> (Remastered straight away: <https://dec3ptor.github.io/StickFighters/remastered/>)

## What's in it

- **Online multiplayer**: quick match, a server browser, hosting your own game (public, or invite-link only), chat (in team modes, names in their team's colour), and bots that fill empty slots and make room as people join. No game server: players connect directly to each other (see [Online play](#online-play)).
- **Nine modes**: free-for-all, team deathmatch, **gun game** and **one in the chamber**, plus five objective modes for two teams: **king of the hill**, **capture the flag**, Battlefield-style **Conquest** and **Rush**, and **Defuse**, played like Counter-Strike: rounds with no respawning, a buy menu and an economy, and a bomb to plant or defuse (see [Defuse](#defuse)).
- **Battlefield-style classes** (switched off for now: add `?classes` to the address to try them): **Assault**, **Engineer**, **Support** and **Recon**, each spawning with its own kit and a gadget on `G` (medkits, ammo boxes, spawn beacons, and the Engineer's **Builder**, which puts up walls and planks and repairs what explosions broke), with a **Loadout** screen for each class's weapons and upgrades (see [Classes](#classes)).
- **44 weapons**: the original eight with their original numbers, plus Worms-style ones like sheep, the Holy Hand Grenade, air strikes and the ninja rope, and Counter-Strike-style ones: the AK-47, MP5K and Beretta (cut from the original game), a knife, and HE, flash and smoke grenades (full list below). **Supply crates** parachute in with random weapons.
- **Kill streaks**: kills in a row (without dying) earn rewards you bank and use with `X` when you choose: a **Supply Drop** (3), a **Sentry Sheep** (5), a **Sheep Stampede** (7) and the **Juggernaut** (10). Bots use them too. Multi-kills, sprees, **first blood** and **shutdowns** get callouts, the match ends with **awards** and a **slow-motion replay of the winning kill**, and every kill you make hits with a moment of hit-stop, a kill-confirm mark and its own sound (see [Kill streaks](#kill-streaks)).
- **The Hammer**: a pickup on every map that lets anyone build. Put up walls for cover, and planks to make steps, ledges and bridges where the way is usually blocked (see [The Hammer](#the-hammer)).
- **Bots that get themselves out of trouble.** A bot stuck in a pit builds steps out with a hammer or flies out with a jetpack (waiting for the fuel if it needs more), and one that can't get to an objective or an enemy builds its way up to a ledge or across a gap, or flies there. A wall in the way (one somebody built, or on a destructible map the map's own) it digs through with the sledgehammer, a chunk a swing, and it digs its way out of a room it's been walled into. With none of those, it first goes and gets a jetpack or hammer it can reach. Hurt in a fight at range, a bot with a hammer puts up a wall to hide behind. Self-destructing is the last resort, a few seconds after it finds it has no way out of its own.
- **Jetpacks and hammers wherever the way can get blocked**: every map has them placed so one is never more than a few seconds away, even after explosions cut routes off (see [The Hammer](#the-hammer)).
- **All 8 original maps**, decoded from the original game's files: Fire in the Hole, Habitat, King of the Hill, Fraghouse, Sniping Grounds, Fatality Zone, Hells Steeple and Two Towers.
- **11 new maps**, from small to huge:
  - **Small** (4–16 players): **Summit** (a stepped mountain, made for king of the hill) and **Pit Stop** (a garage with three rotating hills), and **Crossfire** (bunkers and three lanes) and **Twin Keeps** (two castles across a moat), made for capture the flag.
  - **Medium** (6–24 players): **Foundry** (catwalks over molten pits) and **Skyline** (rooftops with deadly gaps), and **Kasbah** (a desert town, made for Defuse: see [Defuse](#defuse)).
  - **Large** (16–64 players, up to 8,000 px wide): **Frontline** (trenches, a ruined village and the defenders' HQ) and **Citadel** (from the beach up to a hilltop keep), made for Rush, and **Harbor** (ships, container stacks and a gantry crane) and **Valley** (hills, caves and a river gorge, with 7 capture points), made for Conquest.
  - Every map, the originals included, has hills, flags, capture points, M-COMs and bomb sites for all five objective modes. Picking a map in the setup switches to the mode it's made for and raises the bots to its smallest player count.
- **Destructible maps** (setup → **Destruction**, on by default): explosions blow holes in walls, floors and platforms, so you can blast a way through a wall, drop enemies through the floor, or dig into a hill. The sledgehammer does it slowly, a chunk a swing. The bigger the blast, the bigger the hole (a bazooka goes through a wall; the Holy Hand Grenade takes a big bite out of anything). Floors that spawn points, flags, M-COMs, hills, capture points and bomb sites stand on can't be destroyed, so every mode keeps working; neither can jump pads, or platforms a map maker marks indestructible. Blocks that can't be destroyed are lightly hatched. Bots find their way through new holes, and in online games everyone sees the same holes, including players who join late.
- **A map maker** with mirror editing, spawn loadouts, test play, share links and **community maps** anyone can browse and play.
- **Stick Soldiers Remastered**: the same game in 2.5D, picked when you first visit (see [Stick Soldiers Remastered](#stick-soldiers-remastered)).
- **Health, armour, jetpacks and jump pads**, placed where the original had them. Everyone has 150 health (100 in Defuse, as its weapons and money are made for that): guns and melee take half as many hits again to kill, explosions take the same share of your health as they always did, and a sniper or longbow headshot still kills outright. Dead soldiers drop their weapon. Picking up a new weapon only takes it out if you're holding the starting pistol; otherwise it goes in your inventory (the message says which key selects it).
- **Experimental options** (in the game setup and in **Settings**, under **Experimental**):
  - **Bot vision: only what they see or hear.** Bots spot enemies only on their own screen (a view about as big as yours), in front of them and in a clear line. Behind them, they can only hear you: gunfire, explosions, jetpacks, hard landings and running close by. Creeping along crouched is silent. A bot that hears something turns round and goes to have a look, and a bot standing still looks over its shoulder now and then. The default is the classic bots, which notice anyone nearby in a clear line.
  - **Fog of war.** You only see what your soldier can see from where they stand. Everything out of their line of sight is greyed out, and soldiers there (and what they fire) aren't drawn, teammates included; on big maps the minimap still shows where your team is. Sounds from places you can't see show as orange ripples where they came from, or as arrows at the screen's edge. In online games the host's choice applies to everyone.
- **Stick figures animated with the original game's leg animations**. Arms follow your aim at any angle, and the dead fall apart. No original art is used: everything is drawn in code.
- **Bots** that get around every map, go for weapons, health and crates (but not ones someone else will get to first), and fight with human-like aim and reactions: they take a moment to react, flick their aim over and settle, fall behind when you suddenly jump or turn, and only shoot at someone who'd be on their screen. They use every weapon: they lob grenades, call air strikes on you, steer super sheep and set them off next to you. There are 10 personalities and three skill levels (Easy, Normal and Hard: on Normal, a bot standing still hits about two shots in three at a dodging player close up, and under half at 300 px). For huge battles, the bots slider goes up to **4,000** (see [Design notes](#design-notes)).
- **The original game's sound effects**, from the Stick Soldiers Python fan project (see [`web/sounds/CREDITS.md`](web/sounds/CREDITS.md)). The new weapons' sounds (sheep included) are generated in code. The original game had no music.
- **The HUD**: health, armour, ammo and jet fuel sit just above the weapon bar at the bottom, with stamina in a thin strip under them. A ring round the crosshair fills up while you reload (the pistol's magazine, or the wait between shots of slow guns like the sniper rifle and rocket launchers): when it's full, you can fire.
- **Headshots** do double damage (2.5× with the sniper rifle, 2× with the bow), and you hear a hitmarker tick when you land one.
- **Smooth on high refresh rate screens**: the game draws a frame every time your screen refreshes (60, 120, 144, 240 Hz...). It still updates 60 times a second, and everything moving (soldiers, bullets, rockets, particles, falling crates, running legs) is drawn part way between updates, while your gun follows the mouse on every frame.
- **Drawn with the plain 2D canvas** by default, or with **WebGL** (through [PixiJS](https://pixijs.com/)) if you pick it in Settings. Both draw exactly the same picture. **Auto** picks WebGL on computers with a graphics card and the canvas on ones without, where it's faster.
- **Settings** (on the main menu and the pause menu): a full screen with its sections down the side (**Video**, **Audio**, **Controls**, **Mouse & crosshair**, **Camera**, **Gameplay**), each setting on a row with what it does, saved as you change it. On a phone-sized window the sections run along the top.
  - **Controls**: every action's keys, as many as four each, and they can be mouse buttons (middle, back, forward) or the wheel too. Click a key and press the new one (`Esc` keeps it as it was, `Backspace` clears it); **+** adds another. A key taken from another action leaves that one, and says so. **Reset all keys** puts them back. Everything in the game that names a key (the hints on the screen, the buy menu, the menu's list of controls, messages like "hold K to self-destruct", online too) names yours.
  - **Volume**: 60% to start with (100% is as loud as the game used to be). Also on the pause menu (`Esc`), next to **Mute sound** (`M`).
  - **Graphics**: Canvas (the default), WebGL or Auto. Switches straight away, even mid-match.
  - **Crosshair**: its style (cross and dot, cross, dot, circle, circle and dot, T or X), colour (the game's own, a few bright ones, or any colour you pick), size, thickness, gap, opacity, and an edge in the opposite shade so it shows on any background. A picture shows it on a light and a dark background as you change it; **Reset** puts it back.
  - **Aim down sights**: holding right click with a gun brings the camera in a little and further toward where you aim, like looking down the sights (Slight, to start with, More or Off). The sniper rifle's scope still zooms out.
  - **Camera**: how far the view leans toward the mouse (**Look toward the mouse**: 100% to start with; 0 keeps it on you), how long it goes on moving after the mouse stops (**Look smoothing**: Off to start with, so it stops with the mouse; it used to be 330 ms), and how long it takes to catch up with you as you move (**Follow smoothing**: 330 ms; Off fixes it on you). They apply straight away, mid-match too; **Reset** puts them back.
  - **Experimental**: the same bot vision and fog of war choices as the game setup, for the next game you start or host.
  - **Frame rate**: Auto, half the screen rate, or a cap from 30 to 240 fps. Auto draws at your screen's refresh rate; if your computer can't keep up, it first draws lighter (Remastered's auto quality), then drops to an even fraction of the rate (144 → 72) so frames stay evenly spaced, and tries the full rate again later (a few tries, so it doesn't keep flicking between rates). Half the screen rate is a steady every-other-refresh. A cap close to an even fraction of your screen's rate is drawn at that fraction (60 on a 165 Hz screen: 55, every third refresh); Settings says when a cap can't be even on your screen. Moving the window to a screen with a different refresh rate is noticed after a few seconds.
  - **Show frame rate** and **show ping in online games**.
  - **Sprint**: hold `Shift` to sprint, or press it to start and stop. The scoreboard (`Tab`) shows everyone's ping in online games.
  - **Mouse pointer**: your system's own pointer, drawn as the crosshair (to start with), or **locked to the game**; and **Full screen while playing** (see [Controls](#controls)).
  - **Developer**: every number the game plays by. That covers movement (run speed, jump, gravity, jetpack, sprint and stamina, the rope), health and the rules, and every weapon's stats: damage, time between shots, speed, spread, range, ammo, blasts, Defuse prices and how bots use it. It also covers the bots (each difficulty's reactions and aim, how far they see, each bot's personality), pickups, supply crates and kill streak rewards. A change is used straight away (health, the bots' reactions and their route maps from the next match), changed numbers are marked, and a search finds any of them. **Copy changes** copies them as JSON, ready to paste to whoever makes them the game's defaults; **Copy everything** copies every number; **Import** pastes numbers back in; **Reset everything** puts them all back. They're kept in the browser, at full speed (the game plays at 75%). In a game you host, everyone plays by your numbers (sent with each match); in someone else's, theirs, and yours come back after.
  - They're remembered between visits. `?renderer=webgl` / `?renderer=canvas` and `?fps` in the address override them.
- **Game speed fixed at 75%** of the original tuning: movement, jumps, bullets, grenades and fire rates are slowed, while the game keeps updating 60 times a second. Jumps keep their height, so every map stays climbable. A hidden speed picker can be switched back on with `SHOW_SPEED_MENU` in `web/src/config.js`.

## Stick Soldiers Remastered

The first time you visit, you pick a game: the **Original** (black-and-white stick figures on white, as in 2003) or **Stick Soldiers Remastered**. After that you go straight into the one you played last, and the menu's **Play the original** / **Play Remastered** button switches at any time. Addresses can say which: `?game=remastered` or `?game=original`, and `/remastered/` goes straight to Remastered. Links to a map or to join a game open in the game they were made in. Everything that happens in a match is the same in both: maps, weapons, modes, bots, physics, settings and online play. Players in an online game can even play it in different looks; invite links open in the host's.

What Remastered changes is the picture:

- **Scenery in layers.** Each map has a look: a sunset city, night ops in the rain, a frozen ridge, a desert outpost, an overgrown jungle, a foundry, a twilight keep, a war-torn frontline, a harbour or a hangar. Each has a sky (the sun or moon, clouds, stars) and three layers of scenery (mountains, skylines, forests, factories, ships...) that scroll slower and look hazier the further away they are. Dark shapes close up (ferns, rocks, rubble, crates) slide past faster than the fight along the bottom of the screen.
- **Solid blocks.** Blocks have depth: you see the tops of floors below you, the undersides of ledges above and the sides of walls, and they shift as you move, as if seen by a camera. They're textured (concrete, rock, rusted metal, stone, sandstone...) and shaded by the look's light, with a lip of grass, snow, sand or tiles along their tops. Holes blown in them get sides too.
- **Soldiers**: still stick figures, but with shading, a helmet in their team's colour (their own colour in free-for-all), a vest when they have armour, a backpack or a jetpack with a flame, an outline so they stand out against any background, and a shadow on the ground. They still fall apart when they die.
- **Light and weather.** Muzzle flashes, tracers, explosions, rockets, fire, jetpacks, jump pads and pickups glow and light up what's around them. Night maps are dark except where something's lit. Rain, snow, dust, embers, ash or drifting motes fall at different depths.
- **Menus and HUD**: dark glass panels over the map's scenery and a modern font. The HUD is the same as the original's, restyled, with shadowed text so it reads over any background.
- **Quality** (Settings, instead of Graphics): **Auto** (the default) starts on High and steps down a level if your computer can't keep up for a few seconds running, before the frame rate is lowered. **High** draws everything; **Medium** fills the blocks' sides with plain colours and has less weather and a simpler darkness at night; **Low** has flat colours, fewer layers of scenery, and no lights or weather.
- **Map maker**: **Look in Remastered** (in the map's settings) picks the look for your map; otherwise one is picked from its name, the same one every time. Share links and community uploads keep it.

| Look | Maps |
|---|---|
| Sunset city | Fire in the Hole, Skyline |
| Night ops | Fatality Zone |
| Frozen ridge | Sniping Grounds, Summit |
| Desert outpost | King of the Hill, Crossfire, Kasbah |
| Overgrown | Habitat, Valley |
| Foundry | Hells Steeple, Foundry |
| Twilight keep | Two Towers, Twin Keeps, Citadel |
| Frontline | Frontline |
| Harbor | Harbor |
| Hangar | Fraghouse, Pit Stop |

Remastered asks more of the computer than the original, mostly of the graphics card. Everything in it is drawn in code when a map starts: nothing extra is downloaded.

## Game modes

| Mode | How it works |
|---|---|
| Free-for-all | Everyone for themselves. First to the frag limit wins. |
| Team deathmatch | Blue against red, no friendly fire. First team to the frag limit wins. |
| Gun game | Everyone starts with the pistol, and every kill moves you to the next of 16 weapons (pistol, magnum, Uzi, minigun … super sheep, baseball bat). The first kill with the bat wins. Get killed (or knocked off) with the sledgehammer and you go back a weapon; so does killing yourself. Unlimited ammo, no pickups. |
| One in the chamber | A pistol with one bullet that kills in one hit, and a baseball bat that does too. Every kill gives you a bullet. Everyone has 3 lives (1–5 in the setup); the last one standing wins. No pickups. |
| King of the hill | Blue against red over a hill. Your team scores a point every second it has the hill to itself (nothing while both teams are on it). First to the score wins (50–250). On maps with several hills, the hill moves every minute. |
| Capture the flag | Take the other team's flag from their base and bring it to yours; you can only score while your own flag is at home. Kill a carrier and they drop it; touch your own dropped flag to send it home (it goes home by itself after 20 s, or straight away if it falls off the map). First to 1–10 captures wins. |
| Conquest | Like Battlefield: stand in a lettered capture point to take it (about 7 s alone, faster with more of your team, and an enemy point has to be neutralised first). Every death costs your team a ticket, and the team holding fewer points loses tickets every few seconds. Run out and you lose. You can spawn on points your team holds: while you wait, number keys pick **Auto** (the usable place nearest where you died: a point your team holds with no enemies on it, or your base), **Base**, or a point. |
| Rush | Like Battlefield: blue attacks and red defends. Blue arms an M-COM station by standing at it for 2 seconds with no defenders there; red disarms it the same way (7 s). An armed M-COM blows after 14 s, taking anyone close with it. Destroy every M-COM in a sector and the fight moves to the next sector, spawn points move up, and blue's tickets refill. Both teams respawn at their most forward spawn point (nearest the M-COMs still standing), unless enemies are right there. Blue loses a ticket per death; out of tickets with nothing ticking, blue loses. |
| Defuse | Like Counter-Strike: rounds, with no respawning. The attackers (Terrorists) plant a bomb at site A or B; the defenders (Counter-Terrorists) stop them, or defuse it. Money from kills and rounds buys weapons and gear at the start of each round. Teams swap sides at half time; first to the rounds to win (3–13, 7 to start with). See [Defuse](#defuse). |

In the objective modes, kills don't change the team score; your place on the scoreboard is your score (objective points plus frags). Tickets and points to win are set in the setup (Auto tickets scale with the number of players), and **Your team** picks blue or red (attack or defend, in Rush). The HUD shows how things stand, with markers at the screen's edge pointing to objectives you can't see, and big maps get a minimap.

Modes live in [`web/src/modes.js`](web/src/modes.js), [`objmodes.js`](web/src/objmodes.js) and [`defuse.js`](web/src/defuse.js): each one decides what you spawn with, which pickups are around, how damage, kills and scores work, where you spawn, and who wins. Adding a mode means adding an entry there. Where the objectives are comes from the map (see [Map maker](#map-maker)); maps without them get some worked out from their layout ([`objectives.js`](web/src/objectives.js)), so every map plays every mode.

### Defuse

Two teams take turns at attacking and defending a pair of bomb sites, A and B, as in Counter-Strike's bomb defusal.

- **Rounds.** Everyone starts each round at their side's spawn, frozen for 7 seconds while they buy. Then there are 1:45 to plant the bomb. Nobody respawns until the next round: the dead watch their teammates. A round ends when a side is wiped out, the bomb goes off (40 s after it's planted) or is defused, or time runs out (a defenders' win). The result shows for a few seconds, then everyone's back at the spawns for the next round. Survivors keep their weapons and armour; the dead start again with a knife and a pistol.
- **Sides.** Blue starts as the attackers (Terrorists, at blue's spawn points) and red as the defenders (Counter-Terrorists, at red's). After half the rounds, the teams swap sides and money starts again. Level at the end, it's **overtime**: $10,000 each, and a team needs four of the next six rounds (sides swap after three); level again, another overtime.
- **The bomb.** One attacker starts each round with it. Hold `E` (or fire, with the bomb in your hands) at a site for 3 seconds to plant it; you stand still while you do. The round clock stops, the bomb beeps faster and faster, and after 40 seconds it goes off: everyone near it dies, walls or not. Defenders hold `E` by the bomb for 10 seconds to defuse it (5 with a defuse kit); let go and it starts again. When its carrier dies, the bomb drops; any attacker can pick it up (`G` throws it to a teammate). Off-screen markers show the sites, and attackers see where a dropped bomb is.
- **Money.** You start with $800 (at most $16,000). A kill pays $300 ($600 with an SMG, $900 with the shotgun, $1,500 with the knife, $100 with the sniper rifle). Winning a round pays everyone on the team $3,250 ($3,500 for blowing up or defusing the bomb). Losing pays $1,400, plus $500 for each round lost in a row before it (up to $3,400); a win brings that back down a step. Attackers who planted the bomb but lost get $800 more, the planter and defuser $300 each, and attackers still alive when time runs out get nothing.
- **Buying** (`B`, in your spawn area during the first 20 seconds of a round): weapons and gear in columns. Click, or press a column's number then an item's. What you can't buy says why. You carry one pistol and one main gun: buying another drops the one you had, for a teammate. `G` drops the gun in your hands. Walking over a dropped gun picks it up if you've none of that sort.

| | Items |
|---|---|
| Pistols | Platinum $200, Beretta $300, Magnum $700 |
| SMGs | Uzi $1,050, MP5K $1,500 |
| Heavy | Shotgun $1,100, Minigun $5,200 |
| Rifles | AK-47 $2,700 (Terrorists), M4 Carbine $3,100 (Counter-Terrorists), Sniper Rifle $4,750 |
| Gear | Kevlar Vest $650, Kevlar + Helmet $1,000, Defuse Kit $400 (Counter-Terrorists) |
| Grenades | HE Grenade $300, Flashbang $200 (two), Smoke Grenade $300, Petrol Bomb $400; four grenades at most |

- **Guns have magazines** and a limited amount of spare ammo (an AK-47: 30 in the magazine, 90 spare). They reload by themselves when the magazine runs out; `R` reloads early. Guns hit a little harder than in the other modes.
- **Armour** soaks up less than elsewhere, and only covers your head if you bought the helmet (headshots go straight through a vest without one).
- **Grenades.** A **flashbang** blinds everyone who sees it go off (the screen goes white): longer the closer they are, less if they were looking away; teammates too. A **smoke grenade** puts up a cloud for about 24 seconds that nobody can see through (bots neither), though bullets still go through it. The **HE grenade** is a short-fused frag grenade; the **petrol bomb** sets the floor alight.
- **On screen**: each team's rounds won and its side at the top, the round clock (a blinking bomb once it's planted) and who's still alive; your money at the bottom left, with what you just earned; prompts to plant or defuse, with progress bars; who won each round and how. `Tab` shows your teammates' money and the rounds so far.
- **Bots** buy like players do: a vest or a better pistol on the first round, a rifle and armour once they can afford them, saving up (or buying an SMG when the team keeps losing) when they can't, a defuse kit, and grenades. Attackers go to a site with the bomb carrier, smoke off the far side and throw a flashbang in, plant, and guard the bomb; defenders spread over the sites, rush to defuse once it's planted, and fight first, defuse once it's clear. Flashed bots can't see, and smoke hides you from them.
- **In both games.** Defuse plays the same in the Original and in Remastered, and online, players in either can share a match. Remastered gives it its own touches: the dropped bomb glows faintly, the planted bomb's red light blinks and lights up the site (in the dark too), flashbangs flare white over everything near them, and smoke rolls out in soft puffs.
- **Kasbah** (6–12 players) is made for it: a sandstone town seen from the side. The attackers start on a citadel terrace on the left, the defenders in a plaza on the right. Site A is the palace roof and site B the old cistern under it. Three ways lead to them, as on Counter-Strike's classic maps: **Long A**, an open sniper lane over the house roofs that ends in a drop onto A; **mid**, the street, through covered arcades and a courtyard to a catwalk up to A and doors into the defenders' side; and the low, tight **B tunnels**, ending in the cistern. Defenders reach either site 9–11 seconds before the attackers, and moving between the sites takes them 12–14 seconds. Most of the town can't be blown apart (the walls that make the routes, and the floors and stairs they rely on), though crates and cover can.
- Any map can host Defuse: maps without bomb sites get two, around the M-COMs Rush's attackers go for first. In the map maker, the **Bomb site** tool (`X`) draws them.

## Kill streaks

Kills in a row, without dying, earn rewards. They're banked (dying doesn't lose them) and you use them when you choose: `X` uses one, and `Z` picks which when you have more than one. The HUD shows what's banked, and how far you are from the next.

| Kills in a row | Reward | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | **Supply Drop** | A crate parachutes in beside you with a strong weapon (a minigun, bazooka, homing missile, Holy Hand Grenade, air strike...) and plenty of ammo. For 10 seconds only you (and your team) can open it; then anyone can. |
| 5 | **Sentry Sheep** | An armoured sheep with a gun on its back. It shoots enemies who come near it (in sight, within about 470 px) for 20 seconds: good for holding a hill, a flag or a capture point. It can be wrecked: bullets, melee and above all explosions. |
| 7 | **Sheep Stampede** | A flock of eight sheep pours out toward the nearest enemies, a few after each, and they blow up on whoever they run into. |
| 10 | **Juggernaut** | 30 seconds of armour (you take 30% of any damage), a minigun that never runs out and can't be put away, and no knockback, with a glowing outline so everyone knows to run. |

Rewards are on in free-for-all, team deathmatch and the objective modes (king of the hill, capture the flag, Conquest and Rush), and off in gun game, one in the chamber and Defuse, where they'd break the mode; the setup's **Kill streaks** option turns them off anywhere. Kills made by the sentry and the stampede count as frags but not toward the next reward (nor does a grenade landing after you died). **Bots use them too**: the Juggernaut and supply drops straight away, the sentry in a fight or by an objective, the stampede with enemies not far off along the ground.

**Callouts**, in every mode: **Double kill**, **Triple kill**, **Multi kill** and up for kills close together; **Killing spree** (3 in a row), **Rampage** (5), **Unstoppable** (10) and **Godlike** (15), each with its own sound sting; **First blood**; and a **Shutdown** for ending someone's streak of 5 or more, worth a bonus frag where rewards are on. Everyone is told when someone's on a rampage or becomes a Juggernaut, and names show a streak badge from 3 in a row, so there's always a reason to hunt the leader.

**End of the match**: if a kill won it, a short **slow-motion replay of the winning kill** plays first (any key or a click skips it), then the scores with **awards**: **MVP**, **Best streak**, **Most headshots** and **Demolition expert** (most explosive kills).

**Hit feel**: every kill you make freezes the action for a moment (hit-stop; online the picture holds, as the game can't), flashes where they fell, marks your crosshair and plays a kill-confirm sound.

### The Hammer

Hammers and jetpacks lie around every map, placed so one is never more than a few seconds from anywhere you can get to, even once explosions have blown holes in the floors (and hammers turn up in supply crates). King of the Hill's pit has two jetpacks at the bottom. Picking it up doesn't take it out of your pocket: press `0` for it. A green outline shows what a click will do (red: it can't, and why if you click). A click puts up a **wall** (12 × 40, standing on the floor below where you aim: cover for a soldier) or, after a right click, a **plank** (64 × 10: a step up, a ledge, a bridge over a hole); right click again for walls. Each click uses a block: a hammer has 8, and another one adds 8 more (16 at most). It reaches 150 px, not through walls, and a block that would overlap the map fits around it. On a destructible map, aiming where an explosion broke the map repairs it instead: what was there at the start comes back in a 64 px square around where you aim. Nothing goes up inside anyone or next to a spawn point (or, in capture the flag and rush, a flag or M-COM), so nobody can be walled in. Built blocks look like sandbags, and explosions break them, even on maps that don't otherwise break. Gun game, one in the chamber and Defuse have no pickups, so no hammers.

Bots pick hammers up too, and use them for what they're for: steps out of a pit, steps up to a ledge or a bridge across a gap to get to an objective or an enemy they can't otherwise reach, and a wall to hide behind when they're hurt in a fight at range.

During a match the browser's own `Ctrl` shortcuts (bookmark, save, select all...) are blocked, so holding `Ctrl` to crouch never opens one, and closing or leaving the page asks first.

**The mouse feels like your mouse.** The crosshair is your system's own pointer, drawn as the crosshair you set up: the operating system moves it, as it does everywhere else, with its usual speed and acceleration and no delay, rather than the game drawing it a frame or so behind. (Down a sniper scope it turns red.) The view follows the mouse as it moves and stops when it stops (**Settings → Camera** smooths it, if you like). In a window the pointer can leave the game; full screen keeps it on the screen. **Settings → Mouse pointer → Locked to the game** locks the mouse to the game instead: a click locks it, the browser hides its pointer, and the game draws the crosshair, moved by how far the mouse moves and kept on the screen; `Esc` frees the mouse and opens the menu, and going back to the game locks it again.

**The controls stay in the game.** While you play, the browser's own reactions to the mouse are blocked whatever keys you hold (`Shift` to sprint, `Ctrl` to crouch): no right-click menu, no zooming with `Ctrl` + wheel or sideways scrolling with `Shift` + wheel, no selecting text, no middle-click scrolling or pasting, and the mouse's back and forward buttons don't leave the page, whether the mouse is locked or not. **Full screen while playing** (or **Full screen** in the menu) plays in full screen, where Chrome and Edge also keep their own shortcuts, even `Ctrl`+`W`, for the game; hold `Esc` to leave full screen.

## Classes

**Classes are switched off for now**: nothing about them shows in the menus, and every match is played without them. Add `?classes` to the game's address (for example `https://…/StickFighters/?classes`) to turn them back on and try them. The rest of this section describes them as they are then.

**Classes** is a match option in the game setup: **Off** is the classic game (weapons from pickups and crates), **On** gives everyone a class. It starts on for Conquest and Rush and off for the other modes (your choice is remembered for each mode), and isn't offered in gun game or one in the chamber, which hand out their own weapons. Online, the host's choice applies to everyone. With classes on, the map's weapons aren't there (health, armour and jetpacks are), supply crates still drop, and the dead still drop the weapon in their hands.

| Class | Kit | Gadget (`G`) | Other main weapons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assault | M4 (120 rounds), Grenade Launcher ×2 | **Medkit**: 90 health for you or a teammate | Shotgun / Uzi |
| Engineer | Bazooka ×3, Uzi (150), Mines ×2 | **Builder**: walls, planks and repairs (below) | Homing Missile / Mortar (instead of the bazooka) |
| Support | Shotgun (16), Dynamite ×2 | **Ammo box**: half a kit's ammo back for you or a teammate | Minigun / Flamethrower |
| Recon | Sniper Rifle (15) | **Spawn beacon**: you and your team can spawn on it | Longbow / Magic Bullet |

Everyone also has the pistol and the sledgehammer. Medkits and ammo boxes drop at your feet, only your team can take them (one each lying around at a time, for 40 seconds), and they recharge in 20 seconds. A spawn beacon goes where you stand (it drops to the floor if you're in the air); it becomes your spawn choice, and your team can pick it too, with the number keys while waiting to respawn (Conquest's points are still there; the beacons come after them). Enemies destroy it by shooting it (60 health) or blowing it up; if one's right next to it, you spawn at base instead. Your old beacon goes when you place a new one or respawn as another class.

Pick your class in the setup, with `C` while waiting to respawn (your respawn waits while you choose, for up to 15 seconds), or from the menu (`Esc`). Bots pick a class to suit their personality while spreading out over their team, and use their gadgets: medkits when they or a teammate nearby is hurt, ammo when they're low, and beacons near the objectives (they don't build).

**The Builder.** `G` takes it out (or `0`), and a green outline shows what a click will do (red: it can't, and why if you click). On a destructible map, aiming where an explosion broke the map repairs it: what was there at the start comes back in a 64 px square around where you aim, leaving room for anyone standing in it. Anywhere else, a click puts up a **wall** (12 × 40, standing on the floor below where you aim: cover for a soldier) or, after a right click, a **plank** (64 × 10: a step up, a ledge, a bridge over a hole). Not inside anyone, nor next to a spawn point (or, in capture the flag and rush, a flag or M-COM), so nobody can be walled in. Each click uses one of six blocks; one comes back every 5 seconds or so, and ammo boxes give half of them back. |---|---|
| Guns | **Steady grip**: 40% less spread · **Extended mag**: 50% more ammo and a bigger pistol magazine (12) · **Hollow points**: 25% more damage, 30% less range · **Quick hands**: 20% less time between shots and reloading. ## Online play

- **Quick match** joins the fullest open game that has room. If there isn't one, it starts a game for others to find.
- **Host a game** uses the same setup as offline play (any map: originals, your own, or community maps), plus a name, a player limit, and whether it's listed or invite-only. Press `Esc` in the game to copy an **invite link**.
- The **server browser** lists open games as they're hosted, with their map, mode and players.

How it works, with no game server:

- **The host's browser runs the match.** Everyone else sends their inputs to the host 60 times a second, and gets back a snapshot of the whole game 20 times a second (about 1–2 KB in binary), plus the sounds, effects and kill feed. Your own soldier moves the instant you press a key (client-side prediction) and is corrected smoothly if the host disagrees. Other players are drawn about 100 ms in the past, so they move smoothly between snapshots. Matches are numbered in the snapshots, so one from the last match that turns up late is ignored. That moment follows the host's own pace (a host that slows down doesn't leave them frozen), and is a little further back when snapshots arrive unevenly (5–10 updates, adjusting by itself).
- **Players connect directly** with WebRTC data channels. To find each other they use **public [Nostr](https://nostr.com/) relays**: hosts post a listing every 20 seconds, and a player who wants to join sends the host a connection offer, encrypted so only the host can read it. The relays used are in [`web/src/net/relays.js`](web/src/net/relays.js), picked by [`tools/probe-relays.mjs`](tools/probe-relays.mjs), which tests public relays from GitHub Actions (the *Probe Nostr relays* workflow).
- **Limits.** If the host leaves, the game ends for everyone. A few networks (some mobile and office networks) block direct connections; joining then fails with a message saying so. Fixing that would need a TURN relay server, which you can add to `ICE_SERVERS` in `relays.js`.
- **Hosting in the background works.** When the host switches tabs, a worker keeps the game running.
- **Ping.** Each player measures their round trip to the host every 2 seconds and tells the host, which shares everyone's for the scoreboard.
- **Spectating.** A player who chooses Spectate tells the host, which sits their soldier out (no respawning) and marks them on everyone's scoreboard; they stay a spectator in the following matches until they join again.
- **Classes and building.** Each player's browser sends the host their class and loadouts (when they join and when they change them); the host checks they make sense and uses them from the player's next spawn. Engineers build on the host, and new blocks go out with the holes, in one list of changes to the map in order, so everyone ends up with the same map (players who join later get the whole list).

## Map maker

Click **Map maker** on the main menu, or **+ Make a new map** under **Your maps**.

- **Draw** platforms (`P`) and jump pads (`J`) by dragging. Click to place spawn points (`S`, blue or red team), weapons (`W`), health (`H`), armour (`A`) and jetpacks (`K`). Pickups and spawns drop onto the floor below where you click, unless you turn that off.
- **Indestructible platforms** (explosions can't break them when Destruction is on; they're hatched): tick **Indestructible** under **Drawing platforms** to draw them that way, or use the Indestructible tool (`I`) on platforms you've drawn: click one to mark it (click again to unmark), or drag a box to mark every platform inside it (hold `Shift` to unmark). **All indestructible** and **All breakable** do the whole map. A selected platform has its own **Indestructible** box too.
- **Edit** with the select tool (`V`): drag things to move them, and drag the corner squares to resize. Right-click or `Del` deletes, `Ctrl+D` duplicates, arrow keys nudge, and `Ctrl+Z` / `Ctrl+Y` undo and redo. The mouse wheel zooms, dragging empty space moves the view, and `F` fits the whole map.
- **Mirror editing** (`M`): anything you add, move, resize or delete is copied to the other side of a mirror line (purple; you can move it). Spawns can swap blue and red on the other side. **Copy left → right** and **Copy right → left** rebuild one half from the other, and **Mirror to other side** copies one thing.
- **Objectives**: draw hills (`L`), capture points (`C`) and Defuse's bomb sites (`X`) by dragging (or click for a standard size), and click to place each team's flag stand (`B`) and Rush M-COM stations (`R`, with a sector: sector 1 falls first). Capture points and bomb sites get letters A, B, C… (change them on the right). Spawn points can belong to a Rush sector, so the attack and the defence move up as sectors fall. Leave a mode's objectives out and the game works some out from the layout.
- **Everyone spawns with**: pick weapons, armour and a jetpack that every player starts with on your map (in free-for-all and team games).
- **Look in Remastered**: the scenery, materials and weather your map has in Stick Soldiers Remastered (a sunset city, a frozen ridge, a jungle...). Leave it on **Picked from the map's name** and one is picked for you, the same one every time.
- **Map check** runs the bots' route planner over your map. It flags pits you can't climb out of, areas you can't get to, spawns inside walls or over the void, and pickups and objectives nobody can reach. Click a problem to see where it is. Test play won't start while the map has errors.
- **Test play** starts a match on your map with the menu's settings, in the mode picked next to the button. The menu (`Esc`) then has **Back to map maker**.
- **Saving** is automatic, in your browser. Your maps are under **Your maps** in the setup, with an **Edit** button.
- **Sharing:** **Copy share link** puts the whole map in the link itself. Opening the link adds the map to the other player's list and selects it. **Upload to community** publishes it (on the same public relays) for everyone to find under **Community**; uploading again replaces it, and you can remove your own.
- **Files:** export and import maps as `.json`, or as the original game's `.ssm` format. **Open… → Copy & edit** starts a new map from one of the 8 originals.

Falling below the dashed line at the bottom of the map kills you. The map's edges are worked out from what you place.

## Running it locally

Needs [Node.js](https://nodejs.org/) 20 or newer.

```sh
npm install
npm start            # serves the game at http://localhost:8080
npm run relay        # optional: a local Nostr relay at ws://localhost:7447, for online play without the internet:
                     # open http://localhost:8080/?relay=ws://localhost:7447 in two windows, host in one, join in the other
npm test             # game logic: physics, bots, weapons, modes, netcode, lobby (through a local relay)
npx playwright install chromium
npm run test:browser # in Chromium: plays matches, uses the map maker, checks WebGL draws the same as the canvas,
                     # checks smooth drawing above 60 fps, the settings screen, classes (the Loadout screen, the class
                     # picker, building in both renderers), Remastered (the game picker, switching games, matches in
                     # several looks, on the site as it's published), and plays online between three browsers
```

Add `?game=original` or `?game=remastered` to the address to skip the game picker.

There's no build step: the game is plain JavaScript modules in `web/`, and the browser loads them directly. Two libraries are copied into `web/vendor/`: [PixiJS](https://pixijs.com/) 8.22 (`pixi.min.js`, MIT licence), for WebGL drawing, and [noble-secp256k1](https://github.com/paulmillr/noble-secp256k1), for signing relay messages. PixiJS is only downloaded on computers that draw with WebGL.

## Publishing

Every push runs the tests. Pushes to the repo's default branch then publish `web/` to GitHub Pages (`.github/workflows/pages.yml`). Work happens on `main`, so `main` should be the default branch: **Settings → General → Default branch**.

GitHub Pages has to be switched on once: **Settings → Pages → Build and deployment → Source: GitHub Actions**.

Before publishing, `tools/stamp.mjs` adds the release to the address of every module, the page's script and the stylesheet (`main.js?v=1a2b3c4d`), so a browser holding files from the last release never mixes old and new code, and every module is always loaded by the same address wherever it's imported from. A test checks the stamped site, and the Remastered browser test plays on it.

## Design notes

- **Destruction.** The map stays a set of boxes. An explosion cuts a rounded hole (a wide box, a tall box and a square between them, 40% of the blast's radius) out of every block it touches, and each block hit is replaced by the boxes left around the hole. Holes that would leave a sliver along a block's edge go to the edge instead. Collision, sight lines, fog of war and drawing all keep working on boxes, unchanged. The bots' route map isn't rebuilt (that takes up to half a second on big maps): every standing spot remembers where each move it tried went, and a moment after a hole only the moves that went near it are simulated again (a few ms in all). That's done a few spots per game update, so a hole never holds up the game; bots carry on along their old routes meanwhile, then plan again, a few at a time. A test checks the patched route map gets everywhere a fresh one would. Online, the host sends each hole (position and size) in order, and players blow the same holes, which come out the same everywhere; players who join late or miss one get them all.

- **Jump height.** The original's physics constants are compiled into its program, which this project doesn't touch. Jumps were first tuned against its maps (about 162 px, the least that let you climb out of every pit in all 8), then lowered to 60% of that, about 97 px (a soldier is 34 px tall), which plays better. Seven of the original maps work with it. On King of the Hill the pit at the bottom becomes a deliberate trap: you can drop in but not climb out, so nobody spawns there, nothing anyone needs is down there, bots only go in by accident, and the way out is a jetpack (two lie at the bottom), a hammer, or dying (hold `K` to self-destruct; stuck bots fly or build their way out if they can, and self-destruct only if they can't). The new maps were reshaped for the lower jump. Jump pads launch 2.05× their stored "bounce" value. A test checks that every map is fully connected (apart from that pit).
- **Bots.** The bots' route planner finds every spot a soldier can stand on. It then tries walks, jumps (including mid-air direction changes) and drops from each spot with the same physics code the game uses, so every planned move is one the bot can actually perform. In a real match a move can still go wrong (a bot lines up a jump slightly off, or lands on a jump pad), so a move that fails costs more to plan with next time and the bots find another way (for the rest of the match: each match starts afresh). A bot that hasn't got anywhere for a few seconds gives up on what it was doing and goes somewhere else. Standing spots are worked out from where there's room for a soldier, so even a nook a few pixels wide that holes leave has one; a bot that still ends up somewhere with no spot (just before the route map catches up with a hole) tries every jump, drop and walk from exactly where it is and takes one that lands on a spot. A planned move that takes far longer than it should (bouncing on the edge of a jump pad) counts as failed, and a bot bouncing with nothing planned steps off the pad. A bot that can't get to an enemy or objective heads for the nearest spot it can get to, and one that reaches where it last saw someone, and finds nobody, moves on rather than waiting. Pickups behind a ceiling or wall are left alone. Before going for a pickup, a bot works out how long it'll take to get there and compares that with the other bots already on their way (by what's left of their routes) and anyone standing right next to it. If someone will clearly get there first, it finds something else to do, and a bot on its way gives up as soon as someone faster heads for the same thing. A bot that gives way leaves that item alone for a few seconds, and a bot already on its way to an item sticks with it, rather than dithering between it and its objective. Jumps cost a little extra when planning routes, so bots walk wherever walking is about as quick, rather than hopping along flat floors.
- **Bots getting out of trouble** ([`web/src/botescape.js`](web/src/botescape.js)). The route map knows which spots are in a pit (no way back to the rest of the map) and which places a bot can't get to from where it is. A bot in a pit with nothing to do there heads for the nearest spot out. With a jetpack it flies there, once it has the fuel for that height (it fills up on the ground; for a climb higher than a full tank lasts, the jetpack is no help). With a hammer it builds steps: a plank a step up and across, then it jumps onto it and looks again; in a shaft too narrow for a step beside it, it jumps and puts a plank (or, narrower still, a wall) under its own feet at the top of the jump. The route map takes a moment to catch up with new blocks, so after each step the bot tries every walk, jump and drop from exactly where it stands, with the real physics, and carries on building until one lands somewhere with a way on (a long jump up could miss, so it builds another step first while it has blocks). An objective or enemy it can't get to at all works the same way: it walks to the nearest spot it can get to with open sky above (steps can't go up through a ledge), then flies there, builds steps up (toward it first, and never in under a ledge), or lays a bridge across the gap, walking out along each plank and going all the way across rather than jumping the last bit. On a destructible map, a click over a hole explosions made puts the floor back, which makes as good a bridge. With no jetpack or blocks, a bot looks for a wall it could dig through instead: a spot it can get to with a wall beside it (no more than about 100 px thick, built, or the map's own on a destructible map, and none of it hard) and, on the far side about level with it, a spot with a way to where it's going (or, from a pit, out). It walks there and swings the sledgehammer at the middle of whatever's still in the way of its body, nearest first, from head height to its feet, walking on as the way clears, until it's through (the route map catches up a moment later). A built wall takes it a few seconds, a thick wall of the map's own several more. It stops to fight anyone it has a clear shot at. A wall that turns out hard, or 30 seconds without getting through, counts as a failed try. A bot with no way past of its own goes and gets one first: the nearest jetpack (one that can lift it that high) or hammer it can get to, even half a minute away; a hammer lying about is otherwise just something to pick up on the way past, not a gun to go out of its way for. Only a bot with no way out of its own and none to fetch (no hammer, a jetpack that can't lift it that high, no wall to dig through, or three failed tries) self-destructs, about four seconds after it's stuck with nobody to fight (going for something to pick up down there doesn't stop the clock). In a fight at range, hurt or reloading, a bot with blocks puts up a wall a step in front of it, standing still while it does, and stays behind it for a moment.
- **Where the jetpacks and hammers go.** Each map was checked by blowing random holes in it, as a long fight would, and seeing which places get cut off from where everyone spawns, and how far every spot is from a jetpack or hammer. Pairs were added (one each side, on mirrored maps) until every spot anyone can get to has one within about nine seconds' travel, and the places holes cut off can still get to one. A test checks every map stays that way.
- **Bot aim.** Bots aim like someone with a mouse. A bot aims at where it saw its target a moment ago (about a fifth of a second on Normal), carried on part of the way at the speed they were going, so a sudden jump or turn throws it off until it catches up. On spotting someone it flicks its aim over, landing off by an amount that grows with how far it had to turn, then settles to a steady wobble (shakier while it's moving or in the air, and jolted when it's hit). It waits a moment before its first shot (half that when switching between enemies mid-fight), and holds fire while its aim is visibly off. It only shoots at someone who'd be on its screen if it were a player (zoomed out with a sniper rifle's scope), so you're never shot by a bot you couldn't have seen. The difficulty levels set the reaction time, delay, flick and wobble; a test checks they stay in order and miss a fair bit.
- **Floating pickups.** Items sit exactly where the original map files put them, which is often 20–50 px above the floor. You may need to hop to grab some.
- **Huge battles.** Up to about 1,000 bots run at full speed on a typical computer, and 2,000 on a fast one. Beyond that the game runs in slow motion instead of freezing (`?fps` or Settings → Show frame rate shows the game speed). Games with more than 32 soldiers keep a grid of who's where (`grid.js`), so bots, bullets, flames and explosions only check soldiers nearby. Bots look at the nearest few soldiers instead of everyone, and route searches, spawn safety and similar work are shared between bots. Only on-screen soldiers are drawn, bots' names are hidden in a crowd, and the scoreboard shows the top 20. Smaller games don't use any of this, so they play exactly as before. `tests/crowd.test.mjs` checks the shortcuts give the same answers as checking everyone.
- **Smoothness.** Nothing big happens in a single update: route map patches are spread over several updates, at most one bot in 20 plans its route in any one update (others wait an update or two), and route searches run on flat number arrays. The busy code makes almost no garbage, so the browser seldom has to stop and clean up (each stop is a dropped frame): every soldier and every particle has one fixed shape, lookups fill lists kept for the purpose (a map lookup answers with one reused record, nearby soldiers are found into reused lists), stick figures' poses are kept per soldier and filled in place, and distances use a garbage-free copy of `Math.hypot` (the browser's makes garbage on every call). Apart from the planning limit, seeded matches play out exactly as before, which was checked update by update. Drawing sets the canvas up once for all the soldiers, skips particles and stains off screen, sorts the scoreboard only when the order changes and remakes texts only when they change. Remastered redraws the night's darkness only when something in it changes and, without a graphics card, copies a still scenery and the vignette instead of drawing them again. The canvas draws only the part of the map that's on screen. (Keeping the map in 512-pixel tiles and copying them each frame, `maptiles.js`, was tried: quicker without a graphics card, but about 20% slower with one, so it's only used with `?tiles=on` in the address.) The minimap's blocks are kept in a picture too, the world is drawn at whole screen pixels, and the canvas is opaque, so the browser doesn't blend it with the page.
- **Frame rate.** The game itself always updates 60 times a second (so physics, bots and online play are the same for everyone), and frames are drawn as often as the screen refreshes. Each thing remembers where it was at the last update, and frames draw it the right fraction of the way to where it is now. The camera eases by elapsed time, so it moves the same at 60 or 240 frames a second.
- **WebGL.** All drawing code is written for the browser's 2D canvas. `glctx.js` takes the same drawing calls and turns them into triangles itself (with PixiJS's own line and fill rules, round ends and joins included), in reused buffers drawn as a few meshes between the cached texts, in the same order, so the WebGL renderer reuses every drawing (soldiers, weapons, animals, the HUD) unchanged. (It used to hand each shape to PixiJS, which built it again every frame: drawing took three to four times as long and made twenty times the garbage.) The map is built once, and blood and most particles are drawn as one batch each, as they're the most numerous. A browser test draws the same frames both ways and checks they match. Computers without a graphics card run WebGL on the processor, which is slower than the canvas, so those get the canvas.
- **Objective modes.** Everything a mode tracks (flags, capture progress, M-COM fuses) is plain data in `game.modeState`, which the host sends in every snapshot, so players see the same thing without any extra messages. Bots ask the mode where they should be (a hill, a flag, the point they've picked, an M-COM), then go there and hold it: they move about inside the area and fight from there, and on the way they sometimes stop to fight. Roles come from each bot's id, so some defend and some attack, and they spread over several objectives instead of crowding one. Rush defenders guard an M-COM from a few steps away rather than standing on it, so it can still be armed.
- **Classes.** Kits replace the mode's loadout at spawn (`applyKit`), so with classes off nothing changes. Weapon upgrades never touch the weapon definitions: `weaponFor(s, id)` gives a view of the weapon with the soldier's upgrade applied (a bigger magazine, a scaled blast...), which firing, reloading, pickups and the HUD use; it reads through to the real numbers, so it follows the game speed. Gadget timers are written like weapon numbers, in updates at full speed, and stretched by the game speed (a 15 s recharge is 20 s at 75%). Spawn beacons are plain data in `game.beacons`, sent in snapshots; shots and blasts reach them through small calls next to the hit checks and `explode()`, which is left as it is.
- **Building.** Built blocks are ordinary map boxes, marked as built (drawn as sandbags, and breakable on any map). Repairs fill in what's missing of the map as it was at the start (a copy is kept) around where you aim, less whatever's there now and anyone standing there; repaired blocks are plain map again. Each new block patches the bots' route map just as a hole does, and online it goes in the same ordered list as the holes, so block numbers line up for everyone. Bots walk on, around and over built blocks but don't build themselves.
- **Remastered.** It's a second renderer, so nothing about the game changes: `RemasteredRenderer` extends the original's canvas renderer, keeping its camera, objectives, pickups and rarer effects, and draws the rest its own way. A block's back is its front shrunk 7% toward a vanishing point a little above the middle of the screen, and only its open edges (not touching another block) get sides; which edges are open is worked out once per block, and again only near a hole or a new wall. Sides are drawn furthest from the vanishing point first, then the fronts. Each scenery layer is drawn once into a strip that repeats sideways; every frame the strips are copied into a picture at 45–75% of the screen's resolution and stretched over the screen in one go, and when the camera hasn't moved, last frame's picture is used again. Textures are small repeating pictures made in code and laid in world units, so neighbouring blocks join up seamlessly. Night darkness is drawn at a quarter of the resolution with the lights cut out of it. The HUD is the original's code, drawn through a stand-in for the canvas that swaps colours and fonts as they're set, so both games always have the same HUD.
- **Defuse.** The rounds, money, gear and the bomb are plain data in `game.modeState`, like the other objective modes', so online players see them with nothing extra: the host runs the rules and players only draw them. Buying is the one thing players send (a message the host checks as it would its own: money, buy zone, buy time, side). The mode has a few more hooks than the others: it can hold soldiers still or change their input (the freeze, planting), say what `E` does where you stand, decide what you can pick up, and drop what it likes when you die. Shop weapons are views of the usual weapon (`weaponFor`, as for class upgrades) with a magazine, spare ammo and Counter-Strike's prices, so the other modes' weapons are unchanged. Bots ask the mode where to be (as in the other objective modes), when to hold `E`, and where to throw flashbangs and smoke.
- **Same game everywhere.** Offline, hosting and joining all run the same `Game` code. The host records every sound and effect as it happens and sends them on, and players draw trails (rocket smoke, jetpack flames) themselves, from what's in the snapshots.

- **Kill streaks.** The host counts streaks and decides everything, as for frags: players send which reward `X` used, and the snapshots carry what each player has banked, who's a Juggernaut and everyone's streak. Callouts are a separate kind of event (to one player, or news for everyone), and the awards and the winning kill go in the snapshot once the match is over. The Sentry Sheep is a projectile (as sheep are), so it reaches players like any other; its armour and team ride in fields projectiles already had. Durations are in real seconds (20 s and 30 s whatever the game speed); the sentry's numbers are a hidden weapon's, so its fire rate and bullets follow the game speed like every gun's.
- **Tuning (the developer menu).** `tuning.js` changes the numbers `speed.js` keeps at full speed (its `BASE_VALUES`, copied from `config.js` and `weapons.js` when the page loads), then has it scale them again for the game's speed, so a change shows at once and lasts through new matches; bots' difficulty, sight and personalities, pickups' respawn times, crates and kill streak thresholds are changed where they live. It keeps a copy of every number as the game had it, so applying a set of changes always starts from the game's own. Only numbers it lists can change (nothing pasted in reaches anything else), each kept in range (health at most 255: it goes online in a byte). The host sends its changes with each match's welcome, and players use them for that match, since everyone's movement has to match for their own soldier's movement to be predicted; they're back to their own for their own games. The bots' route maps are cached by the numbers in use as well as the map and speed.
- **The replay.** The last seven seconds are kept as they happen: offline and on the host, each update's soldiers and projectiles (as small plain copies) and the sounds and effects made in it; online players keep the host's snapshots and events, which already hold all of that. It plays on a copy of the game made for drawing (its own soldiers, projectiles and effects), so the real match isn't touched while the host goes on sending it. It eases into slow motion (about a fifth of normal speed) for the moment of the kill, with the camera gliding to take in both soldiers and zooming in a little. Games with more than 120 soldiers aren't recorded (too much to copy every update).
